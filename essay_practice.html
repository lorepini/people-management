Block 5 · Compensation
What are the four basic areas to understand a compensation policy and judge whether it fits the organization?
¿Cuáles son las 4 grandes áreas básicas a la hora de entender una política retributiva y de ver si es adecuada para la organización?

A compensation policy is "adequate" only when it passes four tests at once — they're known as the Four Criteria of a Competitive Compensation System. Any pay design that fails one of them creates predictable problems (turnover, demotivation, legal risk, or budget blowout).

  • Internal equity — equal pay for jobs of equal worth. Achieved via job analysis + job evaluation. Fails → resentment, perceived unfairness inside the firm.
  • External equity (competitiveness) — pay at par with the industry. Achieved via salary surveys / benchmarking. Fails → attraction and retention collapse (the case where wages slip 10% below market and turnover spikes).
  • Performance link — pay tied to performance through merit increases (permanent) or incentives (variable, re-earned). Fails → no behavioural traction; pay becomes pure entitlement.
  • Cost / benefit (feasibility) — the firm can actually afford it and the returns are measurable. Fails → budget crisis or unsustainable rewards.
The four criteria of a competitive compensation system. Diagnose any pay system by scoring each one.
How this connects to the Pay Model
  • The Four Criteria are the operational tests of the Pay Model's four policy choices: internal alignment, external competitiveness, employee contributions, management.
  • Above the policies sit the Pay Model's four objectives — Efficiency, Fairness, Ethics, Compliance.
Class examples
  • BAG Grocers · Zero Wage Increase — pay-for-performance dilemma; merit vs incentive trade-off.
  • Circuit City — Caveat Emptor exercise: was the pay change actually the cause of the productivity gain?
Milkovich, Newman & Gerhart (Ch1 Pay Model) Gerhart & Rynes (sorting effect)

Block 4 · Performance management
What can we evaluate about a person in terms of contribution?
¿Qué podemos evaluar de una persona en términos de aportación?

Three indicators capture what an employee actually contributes — traits, behaviors, and outcomes. Each tells you a different part of the story, and each has its own measurement risks.

  • Traits — enduring characteristics (conscientiousness, integrity, drive). Risky to evaluate: bias-prone, hard to change, weakly linked to action. Use only when the trait genuinely fits the job and can be observed.
  • Behaviors — observable actions on the job (how they handle a customer complaint, how they run a meeting). Captured by BARS, BOS, behavioural rating scales. Strong middle ground: actionable, less biased than traits.
  • Outcomes / results — what they actually delivered (sales closed, defects reduced, projects shipped). Captured by MBO + SMART goals. Risk: misses how they got there; can incentivize gaming or short-termism.
Implication for designing PM
  • Combine all three — outcomes alone misses how; behaviors alone misses what was delivered; traits alone introduces bias and isn't actionable.
  • For each indicator, decide objective vs subjective measurement (Aguinis).
  • Quality check: every measure must be relevant, reliable, valid — free of deficiency (missing important content) and contamination (irrelevant variance).
Class examples
  • Vitality Health — moved from 13 absolute trait-like ratings to job-specific competencies (behavior) + SMART objectives (outcomes).
  • Deloitte's reinvented PM — replaced ratings with future-focused behavioural statements ("I'd always want this person on my team").
Aguinis et al. (2009) PM cycle Ch10 Performance Management

Block 1 · Foundations
Explain employee engagement & commitment policies (políticas de vinculación).
Explica las políticas de vinculación

"Vinculación" in HR Spanish covers the two ways people connect to a firm — engagement (their energy in the work itself) and commitment (their bond with the organization). Policies that build them are different from policies that just retain people.

Engagement (Schaufeli & Bakker, 2004) is a positive psychological state with three dimensions: Vigor (energy, resilience), Dedication (pride, inspiration), Absorption (flow). Engaged employees give discretionary effort because the work itself energizes them.

Commitment (Meyer & Allen, 1991) is a bond with the organization, with three components: Affective (want to stay — emotional attachment), Normative (ought to stay — loyalty/obligation), Continuance (need to stay — cost of leaving). The three produce very different outcomes.

The HR policies that actually build them
  • Job design — meaningful, challenging roles (JCM characteristics: variety, identity, significance, autonomy, feedback) → drives Vigor + Dedication.
  • Organizational justice + recognition + leadership + psychological safety — antecedents of Perceived Organizational Support (POS), which mediates affective commitment.
  • ToMo design (Doshi & McGregor) — boost direct motives (Play, Purpose, Potential), reduce indirect pressure (Emotional, Economic, Inertia).
  • Satisfy SDT needs (Deci & Ryan) — Autonomy, Competence, Relatedness → intrinsic motivation.
  • Voice mechanisms (Beer & Eisenstat's Strategic Fitness Process) — let employees "speak truth to power" → boosts affective commitment.
  • Avoid golden-handcuff design — non-portable benefits build continuance commitment, which traps people rather than engaging them.
The aim

HRM should build affective commitment + high engagement. Continuance-only employees stay but disengage (classic presenteeism); over-engaged employees risk UPB (Unethical Pro-Organizational Behavior — lying or cheating for the firm).

Schaufeli & Bakker (2004) Meyer & Allen (1991) Doshi & McGregor (ToMo) Deci & Ryan (SDT)

Block 2 · Job design
What are competencies?
¿Qué son las competencias?

A competency is a collection of knowledge, skills, abilities and other characteristics (KSAOs) that an individual needs to perform a job effectively. Competencies translate strategy into concrete people requirements — they're the bridge from "what the firm needs" to "what the person must be".

Two common families
  • Technical competencies — specific to the job/function (financial modeling, code review, machine operation).
  • Leadership / behavioural competencies — transversal (collaboration, decision-making, communication, customer focus). Usually aligned to firm-wide strategy.
Where competencies show up in the HR system
  • Job analysis — competencies populate the Job Specification (the person side).
  • Selection — interviews and tests assess each competency (situational/behavioural questions, ability tests).
  • Performance management — BARS scales anchor numeric ratings to competency-based behaviors.
  • Training & development — gap analysis maps current vs target competencies.
  • Compensation — competency-based pay (skill-based structures) rewards demonstrated capability.
Class examples
  • Ricci & Verona — define the KSAOs / competencies for the marketing director role; write the job specification.
  • Vitality Health — the redesigned PM system uses job-specific competencies as one of the rating dimensions.
Campion et al. (2011) Ch24 Recruitment & Selection (Armstrong)

Block 1 · Foundations
What is the reference point we must have in any people-management model?
¿Cuál es el punto de referencia que tenemos que tener en cualquier modelo de gestión de personas?

The reference point is strategy — every HR practice exists to implement the strategic needs of the business. HRM is therefore "the design of activities affecting employee behavior to implement the strategic needs of the business", judged on two dimensions of fit.

  • Vertical fit (strategic) — HR practices match the business strategy. A cost-leadership strategy (Ryanair) needs different HR than a differentiation strategy (a luxury hotel).
  • Horizontal fit (internal) — HR practices reinforce each other. Teamwork training paired with individual ranking is a horizontal-fit failure: the practices send opposite signals.

Both grand HRM maps make strategy central:

  • Michigan Model (Fombrun, Tichy & Devanna, 1984) — the "matching" model: strategy + structure + mission tightly drive the HR cycle (Selection → Appraisal → Rewards → Development). HR follows strategy.
  • Harvard Model (Beer et al., 1984) — strategy is one of the situational factors (alongside workforce, technology, laws, labor market) that combine with stakeholder interests to shape HR policy choices. HR also shapes strategy in turn.
Strategy at the center. Vertical fit aligns HR with strategy; horizontal fit aligns HR practices with each other.
How to use this in an exam

Whenever a case asks "design the HR system for firm X" or "what's wrong with firm X's HR system", open with strategy as the reference point, then diagnose vertical fit (HR ↔ strategy) and horizontal fit (HR ↔ HR). It's the cleanest structure for any HRM essay.

Beer et al. (1984) Fombrun, Tichy & Devanna (1984) Beer, Boselie & Brewster (2015)

Block 1 · Foundations
What is the difference between the Harvard and Michigan models — and which would you defend for a multistakeholder firm?

Both 1984 models try to organize HRM as a system, but they begin from opposite premises. Michigan is "hard" — instrumental, shareholder-driven, tightly matched to strategy. Harvard is "soft" — multistakeholder, normative, judged on broader outcomes.

  • Michigan Model (Fombrun, Tichy & Devanna, 1984) — "matching" model. Mission + strategy + organization structure drive a four-stage HR cycle (Selection → Appraisal → Rewards → Development), oriented toward shareholder value. HR is the implementation arm of strategy.
  • Harvard Model (Beer et al., 1984) — "map of the HRM territory". Inputs: stakeholder interests (shareholders, employees, unions, community, government) + situational factors (workforce, strategy, technology, laws). Outputs: four policy areas → Four C's (Commitment, Competence, Congruence, Cost-effectiveness) → long-term consequences for individuals, organization and society.

For a multistakeholder firm (cooperative, B-corp, family firm, public-sector body, regulated industry), defend Harvard. It explicitly includes broader stakeholders and judges HRM on more than financial returns — it captures the legitimacy and well-being dimensions that those firms must satisfy. Beer, Boselie & Brewster (2015) extends the argument: HRM should serve a wide stakeholder set and balance economic, individual and societal well-being.

How to write the answer
  • Open with the hard/soft contrast and the year (both 1984 — they emerged together).
  • Name two outputs each model produces (Harvard: 4 Cs; Michigan: HR cycle).
  • Defend Harvard for the multistakeholder context — cite Beer, Boselie & Brewster (2015).
  • Concede that Michigan is sharper for high-pressure shareholder firms or turnarounds.
Fombrun, Tichy & Devanna (1984) Beer et al. (1984) Beer, Boselie & Brewster (2015)

Block 1 · Foundations
Explain the AMO model and why it is multiplicative.

AMO (Blumberg & Pringle, 1982) is the performance spine of the whole course: Performance = f(Ability × Motivation × Opportunity). The function is multiplicative, not additive — meaning any factor at zero produces zero performance, regardless of the other two.

  • Ability — can the person do the job? Built by HR Flow (recruit, select, train, develop). Tested in selection (cognitive ability, conscientiousness, experience — Judge et al.).
  • Motivation — do they want to do it? Built by Performance Management (goals, feedback, recognition) and by the reward system (Total Returns).
  • Opportunity — does the situation allow it? Built by job design (Simons' Four Spans, JCM) and work conditions (resources, autonomy, support).
Why multiplicative matters
  • A brilliant, motivated engineer in a role with no tools and no authority will still fail. The bottleneck is Opportunity = 0.
  • This is the killer move in any "why is performance failing?" question — diagnose which leg is the zero.
  • The whole course slots into one leg: Blocks 2-3 build A; Blocks 1, 4, 5 build M; Blocks 2 and 5 build O.
Blumberg & Pringle (1982) Aguinis et al. (2009)

Block 1 · Foundations
What is the difference between engagement and commitment, and why does it matter?

They sound similar but they're different constructs measured with different instruments. Engagement is a state in the work itself; commitment is a bond with the organization. Confusing them is a common essay mistake.

  • Engagement (Schaufeli & Bakker, 2004) — three dimensions: Vigor, Dedication, Absorption. It's about the energy and involvement an employee brings to their work today.
  • Commitment (Meyer & Allen, 1991) — three components: Affective (want to stay), Normative (ought to stay), Continuance (need to stay). It's about the bond that holds them to the firm over time.

An employee can be highly committed but disengaged (a long-tenured employee with high continuance and low affective — the classic presenteeism case). And conversely, highly engaged but uncommitted (a contractor doing great work but planning to leave when the project ends).

Why HRM design cares about both
  • Affective commitment produces the best long-term outcomes. Continuance traps people without engaging them.
  • Engagement drives short-term performance and discretionary effort.
  • The HR practices that build them overlap (justice, recognition, meaningful work, supportive leadership) but they're not identical — job design tilts toward engagement, while voice and growth opportunities tilt toward affective commitment.
  • Beware UPB (Unethical Pro-Organizational Behavior) — overly engaged + committed employees can lie or cheat for the firm.
Schaufeli & Bakker (2004) Meyer & Allen (1991)

Block 2 · Job design
Explain Simons' Four Spans of Job Design and the "X" diagnosis.

Simons (HBR) frames job design as four sliders that have to balance. Two sliders supply resources to the role; two demand outcomes from it. A well-designed job has supply equal to demand — visually, the sliders form an "X".

  • Supply side
    • Span of Control — resources the role commands (people, assets, budget).
    • Span of Support — help available from others (mentorship, cross-functional partners).
  • Demand side
    • Span of Accountability — range of metrics the role is measured on.
    • Span of Influence — breadth of cross-unit cooperation the role needs.
Diagnostic crises when the X breaks
  • Crisis of resources — Demand > Supply. Many metrics + lots of cross-unit pressure, but narrow control and no support. The role-holder burns out.
  • Crisis of control — Supply > Demand. Lots of resources and autonomy, but few metrics and little cross-unit pull. The role drifts.
  • Red-tape crisis — heavy bureaucratic supply that no one uses; demand is real but blocked.
The exam move

For any "this role is broken — what's wrong?" question, map the role to the four sliders and identify which is the misaligned one. Then propose a redesign that moves only that slider, not all four.

Simons — HBR "Designing High-Performance Jobs"

Block 2 · Workforce planning
What is workforce planning and what are the action menus when demand > supply vs demand < supply?

Workforce planning is the process of getting the right person, in the right job, at the right time, at the right cost. Operationally, it's a four-step exercise that balances forecasted labor demand against forecasted labor supply. The action menu depends entirely on which way the gap runs.

  1. Analyse the current workforce (skills, demographics, turnover).
  2. Forecast labor demand from the strategy (growth, new products, automation).
  3. Forecast labor supply (internal mobility, retirements, external labor market).
  4. Close the gap.

Layoff criteria (when needed) include: seniority (LIFO), employer status (temporary vs core), skill (keep critical capabilities), merit (performance-based), personal situation (hardship). Defending the criterion choice matters as much as the layoff itself.

Class examples
  • PowerTech India — growth scenario (demand > supply): the recruitment + retention menu.
  • SolidTech — downsizing scenario (demand < supply): the layoff-criteria choice.
Lepak & Gowan

Block 2 · Job analysis
Explain the difference between a Job Description and a Job Specification.

Both are outputs of Job Analysis — the systematic process of recording what a job involves. They sit side by side, but they describe two different things: the job itself vs the person who must do it.

  • Job Description (the job) — title, duties, responsibilities, reporting line, working conditions, tools, environment. Answers: "what does this role actually do, day to day?"
  • Job Specification (the person) — required KSAOs: education, experience, technical skills, behavioural competencies, personal characteristics. Answers: "what must the person be?"

Together they're the foundation of recruitment: without them, selection has no criteria, training has no target, and PM has nothing to evaluate against. They feed directly into the competency model (Q4) and the structured-interview questions (Q12).

Class examples
  • Ricci — write the job description and specification for the marketing director role.
  • Verona — same exercise for a different functional role; compare the KSAOs.
Ch24 Recruitment & Selection (Armstrong)

Block 3 · Selection
What are the strongest predictors of job performance — and which selection tools should we therefore trust?

Decades of meta-analytic evidence (Judge et al., 2007) rank-order the predictors that actually work. The implications are uncomfortable for what most firms do.

  • General Mental Ability (GMA) — the single strongest predictor across jobs. Test for it explicitly.
  • Conscientiousness — the strongest Big Five trait predictor. Use a validated personality test, not gut feeling.
  • Job experience — operates through self-efficacy. Relevant when there's actual evidence of past performance.
  • Structured interviews — same competency-anchored questions and rubric for every candidate, scored by a panel.

Cappelli (2019) is blunt about the rest: unstructured interviews and college grades are weak predictors — yet they remain the most common selection tools. The fix isn't more interviews; it's structured interviews combined with ability tests.

The exam move

If a selection-policy question gives you an unstructured "chat" interview + GPA + a CV review, attack it. Cite Judge et al. + Cappelli to defend GMA testing + conscientiousness + structured interviews instead.

Judge et al. (2007) Cappelli (2019)

Block 3 · Training
Explain Kirkpatrick's four levels of training evaluation.

Kirkpatrick's framework forces training evaluation to go beyond "did they like it?". It rises through four levels, each progressively harder to measure — and each progressively more important to the business.

  • L1 — Reactions — satisfaction surveys at end of training. Easy. Tells you nothing about whether learning happened.
  • L2 — Learning — pre/post tests of knowledge or skills. Tells you what they absorbed.
  • L3 — Behavior — on-the-job application weeks/months later. Tells you whether the training transferred.
  • L4 — Results — organizational impact: ROI, productivity, quality, customer satisfaction. The hardest to attribute but the only one that justifies the investment.

Kirkpatrick is the final phase of the Training Process Model (Needs Assessment → Design → Implementation → Evaluation). The evaluation result loops back to inform the next needs assessment.

The exam move

For any case where a firm reports "the training was well-received", flag that they've only measured L1. Push them to L3 (did behavior change?) and L4 (did the business benefit?).

Block 3 · Selection
What is the difference between Person–Organization fit and Person–Job fit, and what's the trade-off?

Two selection criteria with very different consequences for who ends up in the firm and how diverse it becomes.

  • Person–Organization (P-O) fit — alignment with the firm's culture, values and norms. Soft, broad. Measured with culture-style instruments (e.g. Spencer Stuart's eight styles plotted on two axes: independence-interdependence × flexibility-stability). P-O fit CAN be socialized over time through onboarding and the three domains of socialization (organization, group, task).
  • Person–Job (P-J) fit — KSAOs match the specific job's requirements. Concrete, narrow. Measured via competency assessment, ability tests, structured interviews. P-J fit is HARDER to retrain — you can teach a skill, but at a much higher cost than hiring it.
The trade-off
  • Over-selecting for P-O fit → homogeneity, groupthink, reduced diversity and innovation. Consulting firms have famously suffered from this.
  • Over-selecting for P-J fit → technically excellent but culturally misaligned hires who don't stay (or who poison the culture).
  • Best practice — weight both, deliberately seek complementary candidates who bring something the existing team lacks.

If the case mentions a homogeneous, groupthink-prone team, the diagnosis is usually over-selection for P-O fit. The fix is to add weight on P-J fit and recruit complementary profiles.

Block 4 · Performance management
Explain the difference between performance appraisal and performance management.

Aguinis, Joo & Gottfredson (2011) — "Why we hate PM" — argue that most firms do appraisal and call it management. Knowing the difference is the central diagnostic of the block.

Both have legitimate uses (PM has both administrative uses like pay/promotion decisions and developmental uses like coaching) — but the design choices differ radically depending on where on the dial you sit.

Class examples
  • Vitality Health (Bingham & Beer) — redesign that combined relative ranking + competencies + SMART objectives + common cycle + cash + equity. Pushed toward the management end while keeping appraisal teeth.
  • Deloitte's reinvented PM — pure shift to the management end: weekly check-ins + four future-focused statements per employee.
  • Alex Sander — 360-degree feedback case. 360 works only where culture supports honest peer feedback (Alex Sander = it didn't).
Aguinis et al. (2009) Aguinis, Joo & Gottfredson (2011)

Block 4 · Rater errors
What are the main rating errors in performance appraisal, and how can they be reduced?

Subjective ratings are vulnerable to a catalogue of systematic cognitive biases. Naming them is half the answer; proposing fixes is the other half.

  • Halo error — one prominent trait colors the rating on every dimension ("she's brilliant at sales, so she must be great at teamwork too").
  • Leniency / Severity — rater is too soft or too hard across the board.
  • Central tendency — rater clusters everyone in the middle to avoid conflict or differentiation.
  • Contrast effect — judging an employee relative to others recently rated, rather than against the standard.
  • Recency bias — the last few months dominate the year's rating.
  • Similar-to-me bias — favoring employees who resemble the rater (background, style, demographic).
How to reduce them
  • Frame-of-Reference (FOR) training — calibrates raters by walking them through example performances and agreed scoring. The most direct fix.
  • Use behavior-anchored scales — BARS or BOS reduces trait-based bias by forcing raters to score against observable behaviors.
  • Use Mixed Standard Scales (Blanz & Ghiselli, 1972) — keyed good/avg/poor statements scored via a hidden key reduces leniency drift.
  • Multiple raters — 360 feedback dilutes any single rater's bias (but only works in a high-trust culture; otherwise it imports new political biases).
  • Forced distribution — eliminates central tendency and leniency at the cost of team cohesion (see Q17).
Ch10 Performance Management Blanz & Ghiselli (1972)

Block 4 · Forced distribution
Explain forced distribution and its trade-offs.

Forced distribution is a relative-ranking system that allocates employees into fixed-percentage categories — usually a bell curve. It's the canonical fix for rating inflation, but it imports its own costs.

Pros
  • Breaks rating inflation — the most common PM disease.
  • Forces differentiation — supports clear pay, promotion and layoff decisions.
  • Improves accountability — raters can't avoid hard calls.
Cons
  • Destroys team cohesion — colleagues become rivals competing for the top buckets.
  • Unfair in high-talent teams — a great team is forced to label someone "low" even if no one deserves it.
  • Demotivates — being labelled bottom-bucket has lasting effects on engagement.
  • Can be gamed — managers stuff teams with "buffer" hires to protect their stars.
Vitality Health

Bingham & Beer's case study: replaced 13 absolute rating levels with five relative categories (Top Achiever / Achiever / Low Achiever / Unacceptable / Not Rated), backed by job-specific competencies, SMART objectives, a common January cycle, and short + long-term cash + equity. A reasoned use of forced distribution — defended with competencies and objectives so it isn't a pure ranking exercise.

Bingham & Beer (Vitality Health · case 913-501) Ch10 PM

Block 5 · Compensation
Explain the difference between the incentive effect and the sorting effect of pay.

Gerhart & Rynes distinguish two mechanisms by which pay influences performance. Most discussions remember only the first; their key contribution was showing the second is usually the bigger lever.

  • Incentive effect — current employees respond to the pay structure by changing effort. A piece-rate scheme makes them work faster.
  • Sorting effect — the pay structure itself changes who joins and stays. High-variable pay attracts risk-tolerant high performers and pushes risk-averse lower performers out (self-selection).
The evidence — the glass-installation study

A glass-installation firm switched to piece-rate and saw a 44% productivity rise. Most of the gain came not from existing workers trying harder, but from better workers self-selecting in and weaker ones leaving. The sorting effect dominated.

Why this matters in essays

The killer counter to "money doesn't really motivate" is the sorting effect. Pay design doesn't just change how hard people work — it changes who you have in the first place. Use it when challenged on PFP.

Gerhart & Rynes Ch1 Pay Model

Block 5 · Pay-for-performance
What is the difference between a merit increase and an incentive?

Both are pay-for-performance devices, but they're structurally different. Confusing them is a common case-analysis mistake — and they produce very different long-term cost dynamics.

  • Merit increase — a permanent rise in base pay, awarded for past performance. Sticky: once given, it stays year after year, compounding cost. Rewards past behavior; the future is not contingent.
  • Incentive — variable pay, re-earned each period, specified in advance. Tied to future behavior. If conditions aren't met, it isn't paid.
Concrete examples
  • Merit — "Your annual review rating was 'exceeds expectations'. Your base salary increases 4%." Permanent.
  • Incentive — "If quarterly sales hit €1M, your bonus is 10% of salary." Variable, re-earned, conditional.
The trade-off
  • Merit rewards loyalty and tenure, retains people, but builds permanent fixed cost into the salary base.
  • Incentive motivates without long-term commitment, but can produce short-termism and gaming if poorly designed.
  • Sorting consequence (Q18) — heavy-incentive firms attract risk-tolerant performers; heavy-merit firms attract security-seekers.
Class examples
  • BAG Grocers — wrestles directly with the merit-vs-incentive trade-off.
  • Zero Wage Increase — what happens when neither lever moves.

Block 6 · Unions
Why does Pfeffer argue that unions are compatible with — and can even sustain — high-performance work systems?

Pfeffer's contribution is a deliberate inversion of the conventional wisdom. The standard claim is "management gets the union it deserves" — i.e. good HR forestalls unionization. Pfeffer argues the opposite: even good HR doesn't make unions redundant, and in many cases unions are what make high-performance work systems durable.

The argument in three moves
  1. Union wage effects don't necessarily kill profits. Higher union wages attract more skilled workers, reduce turnover, and push firms to economize on labor via capital investment and training. The result is a feedback loop: productivity rises and offsets the wage cost. Profit hits bite mainly where the firm holds monopoly power.
  2. Unions and HPWS are positively associated. Contracts lock in high-commitment practices (training, self-managed teams, job security, information sharing). Without that contractual durability, HPWS erode under cost pressure — managers backslide on the investments.
  3. Bargaining is power-sharing, not zero-sum. Cooperative bargaining institutionalizes communication and joint decision-making between management and front-line staff. Adversarial bargaining shrinks the joint gains both sides could share.

The strategic upshot is the High Road vs Low Road choice. The Low Road (compete on labor cost) is unwinnable for advanced economies. The High Road (compete on innovation, productivity, brainpower via HPWS) is sustainable — and union backing makes it durable by entrenching the commitments in contract language.

Class examples
  • Ford · Saturn (GM) — union + HPWS coexistence and synergy.
  • Kaiser-Permanente — labor-management partnership delivering joint gains, including lower patient mortality with unionized nurses.
  • Tepperman's — counter-example: tentative deal rejected 23-4 by members; what happens when bargaining fails.
Pfeffer (drawing on Freeman & Medoff)
