§ 0 — The big picture

Two maps, one engine

Almost every framework in this course refines two ideas: how HRM connects to outcomes, and what makes people perform. Anchor yourself here first — the rest is detail.

The engine — AMO

A × M × O is multiplicative: any zero kills performance. The whole course slots into one of the three legs.

The map — Harvard Model

Multistakeholder, "soft" HRM. Stakeholders shape policies → policies produce 4 C's → which produce well-being for individuals, the firm, and society. The Michigan model is the harder, shareholder-only counterpart.

Chapter one · Block 1

01Foundations: SHRM, engagement & commitment

Before HRM is a system, it's a stance. What does the firm owe its people? What do its people owe back?

Block 1 sets the conceptual stage for everything that follows. It introduces the two grand maps of human-resource management — Harvard's multistakeholder lens (Beer et al. 1984) and Michigan's "matching" model (Fombrun, Tichy & Devanna 1984) — and asks you to take a position on each. It then drops the performance spine (AMO: Ability × Motivation × Opportunity), which becomes the structural diagram for the whole course: every later block is, in effect, an investigation of one leg of A, M or O.

Once the maps are on the wall, the block pivots to the psychology of the employee: why people show up at all, why they stay, and why some thrive while others quietly disengage. Here you meet the two constructs that examiners most love to confuse — engagement (a positive work-state with three dimensions: Vigor, Dedication, Absorption) and commitment (a three-component bond: Affective, Normative, Continuance) — alongside the motivational theories that explain why HR practices work at all: Self-Determination Theory, Conservation of Resources, Social Exchange Theory and Perceived Organizational Support.

The block closes with a normative claim — HRM is a system of choices, and those choices have consequences far beyond profit. Beer, Boselie & Brewster (2015) argue that strategic HRM has drifted into a shareholder-only paradigm and call for a return to a multistakeholder ethic. The Ryanair case is the laboratory where the abstract becomes operational: a tight cost-leadership HR design that scores high on horizontal fit but raises hard questions about congruence, well-being and societal impact.

The frameworks worth knowing cold

Harvard Model of HRM Beer, Spector, Lawrence, Mills & Walton (1984) Core
A multi-stakeholder map: situational forces + stakeholder interests shape HR policy choices → HR outcomes → long-term well-being.
  • The inputs — two pillars — (1) situational factors (workforce characteristics, business strategy, management philosophy, labour market, laws & values, unions, technology) and (2) stakeholder interests (shareholders, management, employee groups, government, community, unions). The model insists both shape HRM — strategy alone is not enough.
  • Four HR policy areas (the "what" of HRM) — Employee Influence (voice, participation, power-sharing), HR Flow (in–through–out: recruit, develop, exit), Reward Systems (pay, benefits, recognition), Work Systems (job design, technology, work organization).
  • Four C's (HR outcomes) — Commitment (psychological attachment), Competence (capability), Congruence (alignment of interests across stakeholders), Cost-effectiveness (efficient use of HR investments).
  • Long-term consequences (3 levels) — individual well-being, organizational effectiveness, societal well-being. This triple-bottom-line frame is what makes Harvard normative, not just descriptive.
  • Mechanism — inputs are filtered by the firm's choice of HR policies; those policies produce the 4 C's, and the C's accumulate into the long-term consequences. Feedback loops mean consequences re-shape future inputs.
  • Boundary condition — Harvard is a map, not a prescription; it forces the analyst to ask which stakeholders win and lose under any given HR design.
  • Course connections — pairs with the Beer/Boselie/Brewster (2015) multistakeholder reading; foil to Michigan; provides the C's that engagement, commitment and POS literatures later operationalize.
  • Exam move — the default lens to redesign a whole HR system (Ryanair). Open with: "Using Harvard, the four policy areas reveal…"; close on the 4 C's and the three levels of consequence.
AMO Model — Ability × Motivation × Opportunity Appelbaum et al. (2000); roots in Blumberg & Pringle (1982) Core
Performance = f(Ability × Motivation × Opportunity). If any factor is zero, performance is zero.
  • Ability — what the employee can do; built through HR Flow (recruitment, selection, onboarding, training & development).
  • Motivation — what the employee will do; built through Performance Management (goal-setting, feedback, recognition, appraisal).
  • Opportunity — what the system lets the employee do; built through Compensation, work design, autonomy, resources and supportive structures.
  • Multiplicative logic — A × M × O, not A + M + O. A brilliant, motivated employee in a badly designed job still fails — the bottleneck dominates.
  • Diagnostic use — any performance shortfall can be re-described as an A-gap, an M-gap, or an O-gap, which then prescribes the intervention.
  • Course backbone — the syllabus is literally organized along AMO: Blocks 2–3 build A, Blocks 4–5 build M, Blocks 5–6 build O.
  • Exam move — open any "why isn't this person/team performing?" answer with AMO. End by naming the leg that is binding and prescribing an HR practice from the matching block.
The HR System / Employee Journey Session 1 & 2 decks (course framework) Core
HRM = three activity blocks (HR Flow · Performance Management · Compensation) staged across the employee lifecycle.
  • Three activity blocks — (1) HR Flow (workforce planning, recruitment, selection, hiring & socialization, development, exit), (2) Performance Management (set goals, measure, give feedback, develop), (3) Compensation (base pay, variable incentives, benefits, non-monetary recognition).
  • Lifecycle (employee journey) frame — Recruit & Hire → Onboard → Engage → Develop → Progress → Exit. Each stage is the moment HRM "touches" the employee.
  • Mapping onto AMO — HR Flow builds Ability, PM builds Motivation, Compensation + work design build Opportunity. The system view is AMO operationalized.
  • Mapping onto Harvard's policy areas — HR Flow ↔ Harvard's "HR flow"; Compensation ↔ Harvard's "reward systems"; PM and work design ↔ Harvard's "work systems"; voice/engagement ↔ Harvard's "employee influence".
  • Why it matters — gives the essay an organising spine. Any "design an HR system for X" question can be walked stage-by-stage along the journey.
  • Exam move — when asked an open-ended "describe the HR system" question, structure the answer around the three activity blocks AND the six lifecycle stages — this is the course's preferred scaffolding.
Total Motivation (ToMo) Doshi & McGregor · HBR (2015) Core
Six why-people-work motives — the direct three lift performance, the indirect three destroy it.
  • Direct motives — lift performance — Play (work itself is enjoyable, intrinsic curiosity), Purpose (you value the outcome of the work), Potential (the work develops your second-order goals, e.g. mastery, career).
  • Indirect motives — destroy performance — Emotional pressure (guilt, shame, peer pressure), Economic pressure (do it to earn a reward or avoid punishment), Inertia (you do it because you did it yesterday).
  • Motive proximity — direct motives are proximate to the work itself (cause = the task); indirect motives are distal (cause = something outside the task). Proximate motives are predictive of adaptive performance.
  • ToMo score — weighted index from -100 to +100 (direct motives weighted +, indirect weighted −, inertia weighted heavily −). Predicts customer outcomes in retail, airlines and call-centre studies.
  • Culture as performance engine — high-ToMo cultures arise from job design (Play), strategy and identity (Purpose), career & development practices (Potential), and the absence of fear/coercion (low Emotional & Economic pressure).
  • Theory link — direct motives map onto SDT's intrinsic motivation; indirect motives map onto extrinsic motivation. ToMo is SDT made measurable for HR.
  • Exam move — cite when explaining culture-as-performance-engine; contrast with the extrinsic-only Michigan logic; pair with DREAMS for what high-ToMo workplaces look like.
Work Engagement Model (UWES) Schaufeli & Bakker (2004) Core
Engagement = a positive, fulfilling, work-related state of mind with three dimensions — Vigor, Dedication, Absorption.
  • Three dimensions (VDA) — Vigor (high energy, mental resilience, willingness to invest effort, persistence in face of difficulties), Dedication (a sense of significance, enthusiasm, inspiration, pride, challenge), Absorption (being fully concentrated and happily engrossed — close to flow).
  • Conceptual status — engagement is a state (relatively stable but not a trait), not a transient mood and not a behaviour. It is the positive opposite of burnout.
  • Measurement — Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES-9 or UWES-17); self-report Likert items per dimension.
  • Drivers (from JD-R theory) — job resources (autonomy, feedback, social support, coaching) and personal resources (self-efficacy, optimism). Job demands moderate the relationship.
  • Distinction from commitment — engagement is about the work itself; commitment is about the relationship with the organization. You can be engaged in your job and not committed to the firm, and vice versa.
  • Outcomes — engaged employees show higher in-role performance, OCB, customer loyalty, safety behaviours; lower turnover intention and absenteeism.
  • Exam move — use to define engagement crisply before discussing drivers. Always name the three dimensions; distinguish from commitment in the same paragraph.
Three-Component Commitment Model Meyer & Allen (1991) Core
Three psychologically distinct reasons employees stay — and only one is genuinely "good".
  • Affective commitment — employees stay because they want to. Built by positive work experiences, fair treatment, value congruence, organizational support (POS), and identification with the firm's mission. Predicts the strongest outcomes: in-role performance, OCB, retention.
  • Normative commitment — employees stay because they feel they ought to. Built by socialization, reciprocity for received benefits (e.g. tuition reimbursement), and cultural norms of loyalty. Outcomes are positive but weaker than affective.
  • Continuance commitment — employees stay because they have to: the perceived cost of leaving (lost pension, no alternatives, sunk specific investments) exceeds the cost of staying. Predicts the worst outcomes — people stay but disengage, sabotage, free-ride.
  • Mechanism — the three components are conceptually independent but can co-occur. The same person can have high affective + high continuance ("I love it here, and I'd lose a lot if I left").
  • Boundary condition — strong commitment (especially affective + normative) can fuel over-identification, which links to UPB (unethical pro-organizational behavior).
  • Distinction from engagement — commitment = bond to the organization (target = the firm); engagement = positive state vis-à-vis the work (target = the job).
  • Exam move — argue HRM should build affective commitment; continuance traps people, normative is fragile. If asked "why do people stay?", give the three components by name and rank them by quality of outcome.
Michigan Model / HR Cycle Fombrun, Tichy & Devanna (1984) Key
"Hard," matching HRM: align mission/strategy/structure with a tight HR cycle to deliver shareholder value.
  • The matching logic — mission & strategy → organizational structure → HR system. HRM is downstream of strategy; its job is to match.
  • The HR cycle (4 functions) — Selection → Appraisal → Rewards → Development, all feeding back into Performance.
  • Worldview — instrumental, unitarist, shareholder-driven. Employees are a resource like any other input — to be deployed where they produce most return.
  • Strengths — clean integration, strong on vertical fit, easy to measure and audit.
  • Critiques — silent on employee well-being, ignores other stakeholders, treats labour as commodity, risks short-termism.
  • The Hard ↔ Soft debate — Michigan = "Hard" (people as cost/resource); Harvard = "Soft" (people as assets to develop). The contrast structures most Block-1 essay questions.
  • Exam move — the foil to Harvard. Open a comparative essay by setting Michigan against Harvard along three axes: who counts (shareholders vs stakeholders), what HR does (matches vs shapes strategy), what success looks like (financial vs the 4 C's).
Self-Determination Theory (SDT) Deci & Ryan (1985, 2000) Key
Intrinsic motivation thrives when three innate psychological needs are met: autonomy, competence, relatedness.
  • Three basic needs — Autonomy (volition, control over how you work), Competence (feeling effective at challenging tasks), Relatedness (meaningful connection to others, belonging).
  • Motivation continuum — from amotivation → external regulation → introjected → identified → integrated → intrinsic. The right-hand end is self-determined and produces the best outcomes.
  • Mechanism — environments (jobs, leaders, HR practices) that satisfy the three needs internalize motivation; those that frustrate them produce controlled, fragile motivation.
  • HR practice implications — autonomy-supportive job design, mastery-oriented development, supportive leadership and team norms.
  • Boundary — contingent extrinsic rewards (especially performance-based pay tied to controlling supervision) can crowd out intrinsic motivation.
  • Theory connections — the deep "why" behind ToMo (direct motives = SDT-satisfied), engagement (autonomy/competence → vigor; relatedness → dedication), and POS.
  • Exam move — cite to explain why a given HR practice works. "Autonomy-supportive job design satisfies SDT's autonomy need, which energizes intrinsic motivation (Deci & Ryan)."
Conservation of Resources (COR) Hobfoll (1989) Key
People strive to obtain, retain, protect and foster valued resources; gains compound into spirals, losses cascade.
  • Core principle — psychological stress occurs when resources are threatened, lost, or when invested resources fail to yield gains.
  • Resource categories — object (tools, housing), condition (job security, seniority), personal (self-efficacy, skills), energy (time, money, knowledge).
  • Two corollaries — (1) loss is more salient than gain (losses hurt more than equivalent gains help); (2) resource investment is needed to gain resources and protect against loss.
  • Gain spiral — high-commitment HR practices (training, autonomy, support) provide resources → employees reinvest them in work → performance and well-being rise → more resources flow back. A virtuous cycle.
  • Loss spiral — chronic demands without resources → exhaustion → withdrawal of effort → punishment from system → further resource loss → burnout and turnover.
  • HR implications — design "resource-rich" jobs (Job Demands–Resources model is COR's operational cousin); buffer demands; prevent loss spirals through early warning signs (sick days, micro-disengagement).
  • Theory connections — explains the compounding effect of POS, SET, SDT and engagement practices; foundational to burnout research.
  • Exam move — cite to explain how positive HR practices snowball into engagement, and how neglect snowballs into burnout. "Hobfoll's COR theory predicts a gain spiral when HR practices deliver resources faster than demands deplete them."
Social Exchange Theory (SET) Blau (1964); review by Cropanzano & Mitchell (2005) Key
The employer–employee relationship is a reciprocal exchange of obligations and benefits, governed by the norm of reciprocity.
  • Two exchange logics — economic exchange (specified, short-term, contractual) vs social exchange (open-ended, trust-based, long-term). HRM aspires to the second.
  • Norm of reciprocity — when one party gives, the other feels obliged to repay in kind. Over time, repeated exchanges build trust and felt obligation.
  • Mechanism in HRM — when employees perceive supportive HR practices, they reciprocate with discretionary effort, OCB, and affective commitment.
  • Operationalization — Perceived Organizational Support (POS) is the most-used SET-derived construct in HR research.
  • Boundary condition — reciprocity requires perception of the other party's discretion and benevolence; mandated or formulaic gestures don't trigger it.
  • Theory connections — direct theoretical parent of POS; sits underneath the engagement and commitment literatures.
  • Exam move — explains why supportive HR practices produce extra effort: "By SET (Cropanzano & Mitchell 2005), employees reciprocate perceived organizational support with discretionary effort and affective commitment."
Multistakeholder HRM Beer, Boselie & Brewster (2015) — "Back to the Future" Key
HRM should serve shareholders, employees, unions, community AND government — not only shareholders.
  • The diagnosis — strategic HRM (RBV-based) has narrowed the field to firm-only, financial outcomes, displacing the broader humanistic and societal mandate Harvard (1984) gave HRM.
  • The argument — Beer/Boselie/Brewster call for a return to a multistakeholder, multi-level perspective: HRM must balance economic, individual and societal well-being.
  • Stakeholders to consider — shareholders, employees, management, customers, unions, community, government, future generations. Trade-offs must be made visible, not hidden behind shareholder maximization.
  • Why now — post-financial-crisis disillusion with shareholder primacy; growth of ESG/sustainability frames; rising inequality; questions about HRM's complicity in precarious work.
  • Implications for practice — bring stakeholders into the design conversation; report on multi-level outcomes (the 4 C's, not just ROI); reinstate "employee influence" as a legitimate HR policy area.
  • Theory connections — explicit revival of Harvard; foil to the RBV / strategic-HRM paradigm; intellectual basis for the course's normative stance.
  • Exam move — the assigned reading's thesis. Pair with Harvard to make the values argument; deploy whenever a question privileges shareholder logic (Ryanair, gig-economy designs).
Perceived Organizational Support (POS) Eisenberger et al. (1986); Rhoades & Eisenberger (2002) Key
Engagement and commitment rise when employees perceive that the organization values their contributions and cares about their well-being.
  • Definition — POS = the global belief that the organization values one's contributions and cares about one's welfare.
  • Three antecedent families — (1) fairness (procedural and distributive justice), (2) supervisor support (a proximal signal of org-level support), (3) organizational rewards & favourable job conditions (recognition, pay, autonomy, training, security).
  • Other antecedents from the course — job characteristics, recognition/rewards, leadership behaviour, psychological safety.
  • Outcomes — affective commitment, job engagement, in-role and extra-role performance, lower turnover, better well-being.
  • Mechanism — POS triggers a felt obligation to reciprocate (SET), satisfies socio-emotional needs (esteem, approval), and signals that future effort will be rewarded.
  • Theory connections — the most-tested operational pathway from HR practices → employee outcomes; sits at the SET ↔ engagement ↔ commitment crossroads.
  • Exam move — concrete checklist for engagement redesign. "To raise engagement (Schaufeli & Bakker), build POS (Eisenberger) via fairness, supervisor support and recognition; this triggers reciprocity (SET) and affective commitment (Meyer & Allen)."
Strategic HRM — Vertical & Horizontal Fit Schuler & Jackson (1987); Wright & McMahan (1992) Key
HRM = behaviour design serving business strategy; effective only when doubly aligned.
  • Vertical (external) fit — HR practices match the business strategy. Cost-leadership demands lean, efficient HR; differentiation demands skill development and innovation incentives.
  • Horizontal (internal) fit — HR practices reinforce each other; no contradictions (e.g. team-based reward + individual appraisal would clash).
  • Strategy → behaviour → HR — the SHRM logic is that strategy implies a needed set of employee behaviours, and HR's job is to design practices that elicit those behaviours.
  • Typology (Schuler & Jackson) — Innovation strategy → developmental practices, broad jobs, group-based pay; Quality strategy → high commitment, training; Cost-reduction → narrow jobs, minimal training, short-term incentives.
  • Critiques — risks reducing HR to a strategy-servant (the Michigan critique); the Beer/Boselie/Brewster (2015) reading argues it has crowded out the multistakeholder mandate.
  • Theory connections — sits in tension with Harvard; foundational to RBV-based HRM; the diagnostic frame for the Ryanair case.
  • Exam move — diagnose Ryanair: low-cost strategy → tight cost HR (high horizontal fit); is the vertical fit ethical? "Fit" is a necessary but not sufficient condition — the deeper question is fit to whose ends.
Unethical Pro-Organizational Behavior (UPB) Umphress & Bingham (2011) Supporting
Highly committed employees may lie, cheat or conceal in order to benefit the firm — the dark side of engagement.
  • Definition — actions that are both unethical (violate norms of conduct) and pro-organizational (intended to benefit the firm or its members).
  • Examples — concealing product defects from customers, misleading regulators, fudging financial numbers to hit a target, withholding negative information from journalists.
  • Predictors — high affective commitment, strong organizational identification, transformational leadership (yes — counter-intuitively), perceived in-group/out-group dynamics.
  • Mechanism — over-identification with the firm shifts the moral calculus: "what's good for the company" overrides "what's right".
  • Why it matters — challenges the simple narrative that "more engagement = better". HRM that builds blind loyalty courts ethical risk.
  • Theory connections — qualifies the 3-component commitment model (especially affective + normative); links Block 1 to ethics and governance.
  • Exam move — use to complicate any pro-engagement argument. "Yet Umphress & Bingham (2011) warn that strong identification produces UPB — engagement is not a moral safeguard."
Best Workplace on Earth — DREAMS Goffee & Jones · HBR (2013) Supporting
Six attributes describe the ideal organization to work for — a practical checklist for engaging design.
  • D — Differences — you can be yourself; the firm tolerates and leverages individuality.
  • R — Radical honesty — you are told what is really going on; no information asymmetry between leaders and staff.
  • E — Extra value — your strengths are magnified; the firm makes you more, not less.
  • A — Authenticity — the company stands for something meaningful beyond profit.
  • M — Meaning — the daily work itself is rewarding and significant.
  • S — Simple rules — minimal bureaucracy; no stupid policies that get in the way of work.
  • Mechanism — each DREAMS attribute supplies one or more of the resources / needs from SDT, COR and POS — making it an operational checklist for the theories.
  • Exam move — when asked "what makes an engaging workplace?", list DREAMS and map each letter to a deeper theory (e.g. Meaning ↔ Purpose in ToMo; Differences ↔ Authenticity in SDT).
Strategic Fitness Process (SFP) Beer & Eisenstat Supporting
A structured intervention that lets employees "speak truth to power" about whether strategy and values are actually aligned in practice.
  • The problem it solves — senior leaders rarely hear the truth about why their strategy isn't translating; hierarchy filters dissent.
  • The process — a task force of mid-level employees interviews colleagues across the organization, then presents the unvarnished diagnosis directly to the top team in a structured forum.
  • The "silent killers" the SFP surfaces — unclear strategy, top-down style, ineffective senior team, poor coordination, inadequate leadership development, poor vertical communication.
  • Mechanism — surfaces barriers that hierarchy hides; creates legitimacy for change; builds capability for honest conversation.
  • Theory connections — operationalizes Harvard's employee influence policy area; complements DREAMS' "Radical honesty"; an antidote to the silence that breeds UPB.
  • Exam move — cite when asked "how does an organization actually hear its employees?" or "how do you implement Harvard's employee-influence principle?".
Resource-Based View / Human Capital Barney (1991); Wright, Dunford & Snell (2001) Supporting
People are a source of sustained competitive advantage when their human capital is valuable, rare, inimitable and organized.
  • VRIO test — a resource yields sustainable advantage when it is Valuable, Rare, Inimitable and the firm is Organized to exploit it. Human capital often passes all four.
  • Two sub-claims for HR — (1) human capital is itself a resource; (2) HR practices (the system) are also a resource — harder to copy than any individual hire.
  • Strategic implication — invest in people-system capability; protect tacit knowledge; build "architectural" HR (configurations that competitors can't easily replicate).
  • Critique (Beer/Boselie/Brewster) — the RBV paradigm narrows HRM's purpose to firm-level competitive advantage, displacing the broader multistakeholder mandate. It is the universalist paradigm the 2015 reading attacks.
  • Theory connections — intellectual parent of strategic HRM (vertical/horizontal fit); contested by the Harvard / multistakeholder camp.
  • Exam move — cite RBV when defending HR investment as strategic; then qualify with Beer/Boselie/Brewster to show you know the critique.

The debate to lead with — Hard vs Soft HRM

  • Hard (Michigan · Fombrun, Tichy & Devanna 1984) — instrumental, shareholder-driven, matching model. HR follows strategy. People are a resource to be deployed.
  • Soft (Harvard · Beer et al. 1984) — developmental, multistakeholder, anchored in the 4 C's. HR also shapes strategy and outcomes at three levels (individual, organizational, societal).
  • The reconciliation — Beer, Boselie & Brewster (2015) argue most modern SHRM has drifted Hard; the field needs to "go back to the future" and re-centre the multistakeholder ethic.
  • Where you land — most exam answers should open by declaring a stance, even if you nuance it later. The examiner reads the first paragraph hardest.

The applied case — Ryanair

  • Strategy — extreme cost leadership in European short-haul aviation; lowest unit cost wins.
  • HR design — outsourced cabin-crew employment, performance-based pay, minimal training, anti-union stance, low job security, intense rostering.
  • Harvard reading — the 4 C's show a system high on cost-effectiveness, weak on commitment, congruence and arguably competence. Long-term: societal well-being (working conditions, regulator pressure) deteriorates.
  • Michigan / SHRM reading — high vertical and horizontal fit: every practice reinforces "lowest cost". The matching model gives Ryanair a clean bill of health.
  • Multistakeholder critique (Beer/Boselie/Brewster) — shareholders win, but employees, unions, regulators and the wider community absorb the externalities. "Fit to whose ends?" becomes the central question.
  • Engagement / commitment reading — likely high continuance commitment (need to stay), low affective; engagement (VDA) is low; ToMo skewed to indirect (economic pressure, emotional pressure).
  • Exam move — Ryanair is the all-purpose stress test. Walk it through Harvard's 4 C's, then SHRM fit, then multistakeholder critique, then engagement consequences.

Engagement vs Commitment — never conflate them

  • Engagement (Schaufeli & Bakker 2004) — a positive work-state; target = the job; dimensions = Vigor, Dedication, Absorption.
  • Commitment (Meyer & Allen 1991) — a psychological bond; target = the organization; components = Affective, Normative, Continuance.
  • You can have either without the other — an engaged contractor with no commitment; a long-tenured continuance-committed clerk who is disengaged.
  • What HR should chase — affective commitment + work engagement together; avoid the trap of continuance-only "retention".

Exam moves — how to deploy Block 1 in any essay

  • Anchor any whole-HRM essay on either Harvard or Michigan — declare your stance early. Even nuanced answers benefit from a visible starting position.
  • Cite AMO to explain any failure to perform — it's multiplicative, so the bottleneck is always one of A, M or O. Then prescribe the matching HR practice.
  • Distinguish engagement (state, VDA · Schaufeli & Bakker) from commitment (bond, ANC · Meyer & Allen) in the same paragraph. Mixing them up is the single most common error.
  • Argue HRM should build affective commitment, not continuance — and explain why continuance traps people and erodes performance over time.
  • When asked why HR practices work, cite the mediating theories: SDT (need satisfaction), COR (resource spirals), SET (reciprocity) and POS (felt support). These are the "engine room".
  • When asked about culture, lead with ToMo (direct vs indirect motives) and concretize with DREAMS — meaning, authenticity, simple rules, etc.
  • Use UPB (Umphress & Bingham 2011) to challenge "more engagement = better" — too much identification fuels unethical behaviour for the firm.
  • For any "design an HR system" question, structure along the journey (Hire → Onboard → Engage → Develop → Progress → Exit) and along the three blocks (HR Flow · PM · Compensation).
  • Diagnose any case (Ryanair, gig platforms, professional services) along three axes: vertical fit, horizontal fit, Harvard's 4 C's at three levels — then ask "fit to whose ends?".
  • Close hard essays with the Beer/Boselie/Brewster (2015) thesis — HRM that serves only shareholders is theoretically impoverished and ethically suspect; the multistakeholder view is the field's "back to the future" move.
Chapter two · Block 2

02Designing the role & planning the workforce

Once HRM has a stance, design follows. A great person in a broken job still fails — so design the job first, plan how many of them you need, then go find the right people.

Block 2 covers the four moves that come before anybody is hired. First, design the job (Simons' four sliders; Hackman & Oldham's five characteristics; the four breadth interventions). Second, analyse the job and split it into a description (what the role does) and a specification (the KSAOs the holder needs). Third, plan the workforce by forecasting labour demand and supply and acting on whichever side of the gap you find yourself. Fourth, recruit and select, following Lepak & Gowan's five-step recruitment cycle and Armstrong's six-step selection process — aware that interviews are full of biases and that an offer is also a piece of branding.

The iconic visual is Simons' four crossing sliders forming an "X". The iconic claim is AMO: performance is multiplicative, so a brilliant employee in a badly-designed job will still underperform. And the iconic mantra for the planning half is the four "rights": right person, right job, right time, right cost.

The frameworks worth knowing cold

Simons' Four Spans of Job Design Robert Simons · HBR · Session 4 Core
Design jobs so the supply of resources matches the demand on the role — sliders form an "X" when balanced.
  • Span of Control — the resources the role commands: people, assets, budget, infrastructure. Counts as supply.
  • Span of Support — the help the role receives from peers, bosses and shared services. Counts as supply.
  • Span of Accountability — the range of metrics the role is measured on: narrow (one KPI) to broad (P&L, customer, employee, ESG). Counts as demand.
  • Span of Influence — the breadth of cross-unit cooperation needed to deliver: narrow (own team) to broad (whole organisation). Counts as demand.
  • The "X" diagnostic — when the four sliders cross at the centre, supply = demand and the role is healthy. Misalignment names the crisis: Demand > Supply = crisis of resources (role is asked to do too much with too little); Supply > Demand = crisis of control (people empire-build because they have nothing to deliver).
  • How to widen each span — Control: give more headcount/budget. Support: change incentives, build matrix relationships, add internal services. Accountability: change the scorecard. Influence: set stretch goals that force cross-unit cooperation.
  • Exam move — the single central tool of Block 2. Map any "broken role" question to the four sliders, name the missing supply or excess demand, then prescribe the adjustment. Use it on Ricci's Sales Director, Verona's role, or any role-design vignette.
Job Characteristics Model / MPS Hackman & Oldham (1976/1980) · Session 5 Core
Five job features create three psychological states that drive motivation, satisfaction and performance.
  • Five core characteristics — Skill variety (range of skills used), Task identity (do you see a whole piece of work end-to-end?), Task significance (does it matter to others?), Autonomy (discretion in how/when), Feedback (does the work itself tell you how you're doing?).
  • Three critical psychological states — the first three characteristics produce experienced meaningfulness; autonomy produces experienced responsibility; feedback produces knowledge of results.
  • MPS formula — MPS = ((Variety + Identity + Significance) / 3) × Autonomy × Feedback. Multiplicative on the right: a zero in autonomy or feedback zeroes the whole score, no matter how varied or significant the work.
  • Outcomes — internal work motivation, growth satisfaction, general job satisfaction, work effectiveness. Moderated by Growth Need Strength (GNS) — people with low GNS get little lift from enrichment.
  • Exam move — use JCM to argue why enrichment beats narrow specialisation; quantify with MPS; pair with AMO to explain that redesigning the job (O) can rescue performance more than training (A) or incentives (M).
AMO — Ability · Motivation · Opportunity Blumberg & Pringle (1982) · Session 4 Key
Performance is multiplicative — a great person in a bad job still fails.
  • The equation — Performance = f(Ability × Motivation × Opportunity). All three must be present; a zero anywhere zeroes performance.
  • Ability — knowledge, skill, capacity to do the work (lifted by selection, training, development).
  • Motivation — will to do the work (lifted by performance management, rewards, recognition, design).
  • Opportunity — the chance to perform (lifted by job design, resources, workflows, autonomy — this is where the job, not the person, lives).
  • Why it matters for Block 2 — AMO is the philosophical anchor of the whole block: if you suspect "O" is the bottleneck, you redesign the job before you retrain or replace the person.
  • Exam move — defend any job-redesign intervention by citing AMO. "Per Blumberg & Pringle (1982), performance is multiplicative; with adequate A and M, the bottleneck must be O — the job itself."
Job Breadth Interventions Session 5 deck (drawing on Hackman, Herzberg, Lawler) Key
Four levers to widen or deepen a job — pick by what's blocking motivation.
  • Job Enrichment (vertical) — add autonomy, responsibility, decision rights, challenge. Solves experienced meaningfulness and responsibility gaps. Roots in Herzberg's two-factor theory.
  • Job Enlargement (horizontal) — add more tasks at the same level. Solves skill variety and boredom, but does not by itself add responsibility. Risk: a wider but still-boring job.
  • Job Rotation — move people across functions or roles on a schedule. Builds skill variety, broadens perspective, builds bench. Risk: shallow expertise, repeated learning curves.
  • High-Performance Work Design — bundle: skilled, team-based, autonomous work units with supportive HR systems (selection, training, gain-sharing). Highest impact, highest cost.
  • How to choose — diagnose with JCM. Low autonomy → enrich. Low variety → enlarge or rotate. Low everything → high-performance redesign.
  • Exam move — never just say "enrich the job". Name the lever, link it to the JCM characteristic it fixes, and warn about the trade-off.
Stretch Goals — Comfort vs Stretch Zone Simons (HBR) · Session 4 Supporting
Goal difficulty calibrates Span of Influence — stretch goals force cross-unit cooperation.
  • Comfort zone — attainable, routine goals. Provide stability but stop people reaching across silos for help.
  • Stretch zone — near-impossible to hit alone. Force the role to draw on others — widening Span of Influence.
  • Mechanism — in Simons' logic, you don't just describe influence on an org chart; you set it by calibrating goal difficulty.
  • Exam move — if a role's Span of Influence is too narrow, the fix is rarely a reorg — it's a harder goal that obliges cooperation.
RACI Framework Session 4 deck (classic project-management matrix) Key
Roles-and-responsibilities matrix that eliminates "who owns this?" friction in any redesigned job.
  • R — Responsible — does the work. Can be many people per task.
  • A — Accountable — signs off, owns the outcome. Only one A per task — this is the load-bearing rule.
  • C — Consulted — two-way input before the decision is taken.
  • I — Informed — one-way notification after the decision.
  • How to use — lay tasks down the rows, roles across the columns, fill each cell with R/A/C/I. Spot duplicate A's (conflict) or missing A's (orphans).
  • Exam move — cite RACI when redesigning jobs or delegating, and when a case shows decision paralysis. Pair with Simons: RACI sharpens Accountability and Influence at the same time.
"Span of control plus span of support gives you the resources side. Span of accountability plus span of influence gives you the demand side. Balance them and the four sliders form an X — that's a well-designed job." Robert Simons · HBR · on the four spans
Competency Model / KSAOs Campion et al. (2011) · Session 5 Core
A competency = the bundle of knowledge, skills, abilities and other characteristics that produce effective performance.
  • K — Knowledge — what you know (facts, frameworks, procedures; e.g. tax law, GAAP, Spanish labour code).
  • S — Skills — what you can do (proficiency at a task; e.g. SQL, negotiation, public speaking).
  • A — Abilities — underlying cognitive or physical capacity (e.g. reasoning, numerical ability, manual dexterity, stamina).
  • O — Other characteristics — personality, motives, values, traits (e.g. conscientiousness, integrity, customer orientation).
  • Two families — technical competencies (job-specific) + leadership / behavioural competencies (cross-cutting: communication, teamwork, decision-making).
  • Why it matters — KSAOs are the bridge from job design to selection: the description names the duties, the specification translates them into KSAOs, and selection methods test for those KSAOs.
  • Exam move — cite Campion et al. (2011) when defending a competency-based job spec; in a case like Ricci, list 4–6 critical KSAOs for the Sales Director and tie each to a duty in the description.
Job Analysis — Job Description vs Job Specification Sessions 5–6 Core
Systematic process splitting what the job is from what the holder must be.
  • Job Description (the job) — title, purpose, reporting line, key duties & responsibilities, working conditions, environment, scope (budget, headcount). Answers "what does this role do?"
  • Job Specification (the person) — education, experience, KSAOs, personal characteristics, sometimes physical requirements. Answers "what must the holder be?"
  • Process — observe / interview / question incumbents and supervisors, document duties, distil KSAOs, validate with stakeholders, rewrite periodically as the job evolves.
  • Why both are needed — without a JD you can't write a JS; without a JS recruitment has no screening criteria and selection has no validity benchmark.
  • Common mistakes — mixing duties into the spec ("must do X" belongs in JD); listing KSAOs without proof they predict performance; copying old JDs that no longer match the role.
  • Exam move — if asked "difference between JD and JS", lead with one sentence: "the description describes the job, the specification specifies the person" — then unpack both columns.
Workforce Planning — Labour Demand vs Supply Session 6 deck Core
Right person, right job, right time, right cost — achieved by balancing forecast labour demand against forecast labour supply.
  • The four steps — (1) analyse the current workforce (headcount, skills, demographics, turnover); (2) forecast future demand driven by strategy, growth, new products, technology; (3) forecast future supply from internal movements + external labour market; (4) close the gap.
  • If Demand > Supply (growth case) — external recruitment, training/upskilling, retention bonuses, overtime, outsourcing, automation, raising starting salaries.
  • If Demand < Supply (downsize case) — hiring freeze, internal redeployment, voluntary exit programmes (early retirement, buy-outs), reduced hours, and layoffs as a last resort.
  • The four "rights" — right person (KSAOs), right job (design), right time (planning horizon), right cost (budget). Quote this mantra in any planning answer.
  • Why step zero matters — analysing current workforce first prevents the common mistake of solving for a gap that doesn't exist (e.g. firing people whose skills will be needed next quarter).
  • Exam move — name the gap direction first, then pull from the matching menu. Frames PowerTech India (demand > supply) and SolidTech (demand < supply).
"Workforce planning aims to have the right person, in the right job, at the right time, at the right cost — by closing the gap between forecast labour demand and forecast labour supply." Session 6 deck · the planner's mantra
Recruitment Process Model Lepak & Gowan · Session 6 Core
A cycle — plan, set strategy, evaluate sources, implement methods, feed back and control.
  • Step 1 — HR planning — forecast the gap (output of workforce planning).
  • Step 2 — Recruitment strategy — internal vs external, full-time vs contract, employer-brand positioning, budget, timeline.
  • Step 3 — Evaluate sources — internal promotions, referrals, agencies, job boards, social media, campus, head-hunters — rank by cost, quality, time-to-fill, fit.
  • Step 4 — Implement methods — write JD/JS, draft the advert, target the right channels, manage applications.
  • Step 5 — Feedback & control — measure yield ratios, time-to-fill, quality-of-hire, cost-per-hire; loop back into next cycle's strategy.
  • Internal sources — promotions, lateral moves, referrals, internal job postings. Pros: cheap, fast, low risk (you have performance data), motivates existing staff, fits culture. Cons: creates a downstream vacancy (the "domino"), risk of stale thinking, internal politics.
  • External sources — ads, agencies, social media (LinkedIn), campus, head-hunters, walk-ins. Pros: fresh skills, new perspective, broader pool. Cons: costly, slow, less performance data, onboarding burden.
  • Exam move — structure any "how should we fill role X" answer as the five steps; then justify internal vs external by the case's urgency, budget and capability gap.
Employer Brand / Employee Value Proposition Session 6 deck Supporting
Recruitment is a two-way exchange — the firm sells itself just as candidates sell themselves.
  • Employer brand — the firm's reputation as a place to work (distinct from its consumer or corporate brand). Shaped by current employees, alumni, glassdoor reviews, candidate experience.
  • EVP — Employee Value Proposition — the bundle the employee receives in return for their work: pay, benefits, growth, culture, purpose, brand, location, flexibility.
  • Why it matters — in tight labour markets (Demand > Supply) the candidate chooses you, not vice-versa. Employer-of-choice firms enjoy larger applicant pools, lower cost-per-hire and better fit.
  • Trap — over-promising in the brand creates a "psychological-contract breach" on day one — new hires quit fast when reality bites.
  • Exam move — cite when discussing why some firms attract better applicants at the same wage, or when justifying investment in glassdoor management, alumni networks, or candidate experience.
Selection Process Model — six steps Armstrong · Session 6 Core
Six sequential steps from application to offer — each step a filter, each filter must be valid and reliable.
  • 1. Application forms / CV screening — verifies basic qualifications and experience; first knock-out filter.
  • 2. Selection tests — cognitive-ability tests (highest predictive validity), personality tests (Big Five), work-sample tests, situational-judgement tests, assessment centres.
  • 3. Interviews — structured (high validity) or unstructured (low validity); situational (hypotheticals for inexperienced candidates) or behavioural (past behaviour for experienced — use STAR).
  • 4. References — verify prior performance and conduct; legal limits in many jurisdictions.
  • 5. Physical examination — only where job-related (safety-critical roles, aviation, military). Subject to disability-discrimination law.
  • 6. Offer — salary, benefits, start date, conditional clauses; the moment the firm "wins" the candidate or loses them to a competing offer.
  • Predictive validity hierarchy — structured interviews + cognitive-ability tests + work samples > unstructured interviews + reference letters + GPA. Cite Cappelli (2019, HBR) on the weakness of unstructured interviews.
  • Exam move — the spine of any selection essay. Map the six steps, then defend the choice of methods at steps 2–3 by predictive validity.
Situational vs Behavioural Interviewing + STAR Latham & Sue-Chan (1996) · Session 6 Key
Two structured-question typologies; STAR structures the answers.
  • Situational interview — "What would you do if a key client demanded a 20% discount?" Tests intentions, cognitive ability, problem-solving. Best for inexperienced candidates (no track record to ask about).
  • Behavioural interview — "Tell me about a time when you actually dealt with a difficult client." Past behaviour is the best predictor of future behaviour. Best for experienced candidates.
  • STAR — the answer structure — Situation (set the scene briefly), Task (what challenge faced you?), Action (what did you — not "we" — actually do?), Result (quantifiable outcome + what you learned).
  • Why STAR boosts validity — forces specifics, prevents waffle, makes responses comparable across candidates.
  • Critical rule — the "A" must be in the first person singular. "We launched the product" doesn't prove the candidate did anything; "I led the pricing analysis and convinced the steering committee" does.
  • Exam move — pick the typology by candidate experience; defend both via Latham & Sue-Chan (1996); insist on STAR to lift behavioural-interview validity.
Selection-Interview Biases Session 6 deck Supporting
Two named cognitive biases that wreck interview validity.
  • Contrast effect — judge a candidate against the people interviewed just before, not against the spec. A mediocre candidate after three weak ones suddenly looks brilliant.
  • Similar-to-me effect — favour candidates who resemble the interviewer in background, school, hobbies, demographics. Drives homogeneity and discrimination.
  • Other named biases worth flagging — halo effect (one strong trait colours all others), first-impression bias, leniency, central-tendency.
  • Defences — structured interviews with predetermined questions and scoring rubrics, panel interviews with diverse panellists, calibrated scoring sessions, training on biases.
  • Exam move — when critiquing fairness or defending structured interviews, name both biases by name and prescribe the panel + rubric defence.
Layoff Selection Criteria Session 6 deck (SolidTech case) Supporting
Five bases for choosing who exits when Demand < Supply.
  • Seniority — "last in, first out". Easiest to defend legally and to a union; but loses recent hires (often the cheapest and freshest-skilled).
  • Employer status — cut temporary, contract, or part-time staff before core employees.
  • Skill — keep critical capabilities regardless of tenure. Hard skills (engineering, regulated) win over generic.
  • Merit — performance-based; lowest-rated go first. Requires reliable performance data — otherwise opens discrimination claims.
  • Personal situation — hardship considerations (sole earners, disability, dependants). Adds humanity; risks favouritism.
  • The right answer is almost never one criterion — firms usually weight several (e.g. skill 50%, merit 30%, seniority 20%) and document the formula.
  • Exam move — for a layoff vignette, name the criteria, propose a weighted blend, defend with legality + business continuity + fairness. SolidTech is the canonical case.

The cases — what each one teaches

  • Ricci (Distribuciones Ricci, Mestre) — family-business transition; write the JD and JS for the new Sales Director and select 4–6 critical competencies (KSAOs) from the firm's competency catalogue. Pure Block-2 mechanics: JD ↔ JS ↔ competencies ↔ selection criteria.
  • Verona — sister exercise: same JD/JS exercise applied to a different role. Tests whether you can transfer the Ricci method without copy-pasting the answer.
  • PowerTech India — growth scenario, Demand > Supply: an Indian manufacturer needs to scale headcount fast. Frame with the workforce-planning model; pull from the growth menu (hire externally, upskill, retention bonuses, employer-brand investment); use Simons to check the redesigned roles don't break under the load.
  • SolidTech — downsize scenario, Demand < Supply: shrinking firm must lay off. Apply the layoff-selection criteria, build a weighted formula, and defend it on legal + capability + fairness grounds.
  • Fuzzme! (simulation, also referenced in Block 3) — lets you experiment with selection methods: cognitive-ability test, personality (Big Five), structured interview, work sample. Best taken after you've internalised the validity hierarchy.

Exam moves

  • Diagnose any "broken role" question by mapping it to Simons' four sliders — name the missing supply (control or support) or the excess demand (accountability or influence). It is the highest-leverage Block-2 move.
  • Use the Job Characteristics Model to defend job enrichment over training or new hires when motivation is the bottleneck — cite Hackman & Oldham and the MPS formula.
  • Anchor every job-design answer on AMO (Blumberg & Pringle, 1982): performance is multiplicative, so the bottleneck must be A, M, or O — if it's O, redesign the job.
  • "Job Description = the job; Job Specification = the person" — one sentence, never confuse them. Use Campion et al.'s KSAOs to fill the spec.
  • For any workforce-planning case, name the gap direction first (Demand > or < Supply), then pull from the matching action menu. Quote "right person, right job, right time, right cost".
  • Structure recruitment answers around Lepak & Gowan's five steps; structure selection answers around Armstrong's six steps. Defend method choice with the predictive-validity hierarchy.
  • Pick situational interviews for inexperienced candidates, behavioural for experienced — cite Latham & Sue-Chan (1996). Insist STAR's "A" is personal ("I") not collective ("we").
  • Cite Cappelli (2019, HBR) to reject unstructured interviews and GPA as weak predictors; name the contrast and similar-to-me biases when critiquing fairness.
  • For a layoff case, never pick a single criterion — propose a weighted blend across seniority, status, skill, merit and personal hardship, and defend the weights.
  • Use RACI to clean up role-design and decision-rights answers; remember one A per task, otherwise nothing gets owned.
Chapter three · Block 3

03From candidate to colleague — selection, socialization, training & development

Now you fill the role. The governing principle: hire for what you can't train, and train what you can — culture fit can be socialized, skill mismatch usually can't, and the tools most firms trust (unstructured interviews, GPA, gut feel) are precisely the ones the meta-analyses dismiss.

Block 3 carries the workforce-planning logic of Block 2 over the finish line. You inherit an open seat with a job description, a person specification and a sourcing channel; now you must measure candidates, decide on one, integrate them into the firm, and keep developing them. The arc runs Armstrong (the recruitment-selection spine) → Judge et al. (what actually predicts performance) → Big Five and structured interviews (how you measure it) → P–O vs P–J fit and Spencer Stuart (how culture enters the call) → Three Domains of Socialization (onboarding) → Training Process Model and Kirkpatrick (the learning cycle that loops back into needs).

Two through-lines tie it all together. First, validity beats popularity: Cappelli's 2019 verdict is that the most widely used techniques (chat-style interviews, college grades) are the weakest predictors; the strongest predictors (cognitive ability, conscientiousness, structured interviews) are systematically underused. Second, socialization is the bridge between selection and performance: a great hire who is dropped into a role with no organization, group or task onboarding will underperform, and you'll wrongly blame the selection process. The frameworks below give you the language to diagnose, defend and redesign each step.

Recruitment & Selection Process Armstrong · Handbook of HRM Practice (Ch24) Core spine
The end-to-end arc from defining a vacancy to handing over a signed offer — the skeleton on which every other Block 3 framework hangs.
  • Step 1 · Define requirements — write the job description (duties, reporting line, environment) and the person specification (KSAOs, qualifications, traits). Without both, selection has no criteria to score against.
  • Step 2 · Attract candidates — choose internal vs external sources, build the employer brand and EVP, design the advertisement, and protect the funnel against bias.
  • Step 3 · Process applications — CV screening, shortlisting, pre-employment tests. Most bias enters here, especially when scanners or recruiters apply un-validated heuristics.
  • Step 4 · Select — structured interviews, ability and personality tests, work samples, assessment centres. Multiple methods reduce single-method error.
  • Step 5 · Decision — combine scores using a defensible rule (compensatory or multiple-hurdles), not gut feel.
  • Step 6 · References & checks — confirm employment, credentials, right-to-work; legally bounded in most jurisdictions.
  • Step 7 · Offer & negotiation — salary band, start date, relocation, contingent terms.
  • Step 8 · Onboarding — hands off into the Three Domains of Socialization.
  • Three recurring problems — shortages (no candidates pass the bar), mismatched expectations (job vs reality), and biased screening (homogeneous shortlists).
  • Root-cause map for the three problems — shortages usually trace to an unrealistic person specification, a weak employer brand, or the wrong sourcing channel. Mismatched expectations usually trace to a fluffy job description or an inflated EVP. Biased screening usually traces to unstructured early stages and to over-reliance on referrals.
  • Funnel metrics worth tracking — applications per role, pass-through rate at each stage, time-to-fill, cost-per-hire, quality-of-hire (12-month performance correlation), diversity at each stage. The funnel data is where the eight-step arc becomes measurable.
  • Step-to-framework crosswalk — Step 1: job description vs specification, Spencer Stuart axes for cultural requirements. Step 2: internal vs external sources, employer brand and EVP. Step 3: validity and reliability of screening tools. Step 4: Big Five, GMA tests, structured interviews, situational vs behavioural questions, STAR. Step 5: Fisher, Schoenfeldt & Shaw's Phase 2 (decision rules). Step 8: Three Domains of Socialization + Socialization Strategies.
  • Exam move — use this arc as the spine of any recruitment essay; map each subsequent framework onto its step (e.g. Big Five at Step 4, Spencer Stuart at Step 1 or 5, Three Domains at Step 8). The crosswalk above is the single most reusable structure in Block 3.
Predictor hierarchy Judge, Higgins, Thoresen & Barrick · 2007 meta-analytic path model Core evidence
Decades of validity coefficients rank-ordered into a single hierarchy of "what works" — and what most firms still over-use.
  • Strongest predictors — general mental ability (GMA), conscientiousness, and relevant prior experience. These three carry most of the explained variance in job performance across roles and industries.
  • Mediator — self-efficacy. Experience and conscientiousness translate into performance partly because they build the candidate's belief that they can succeed on this task.
  • Weakest defensible predictors — college grades, years of education, unstructured "chat" interviews, and personality dimensions other than conscientiousness when used alone.
  • Implication for design — every selection battery should include a validated cognitive-ability test and a structured behavioural component; personality testing should focus on conscientiousness or use the Big Five as a tie-breaker, not a primary filter.
  • Why firms ignore it — cognitive testing feels uncomfortable (legal anxiety, candidate experience), unstructured interviews feel intuitive, and GPA is a free signal — even if it correlates poorly with performance two years in.
  • Reading the path model — Judge et al.'s contribution is not just the ordering but the path: GMA and conscientiousness predict performance both directly and indirectly via self-efficacy, while experience operates almost entirely through self-efficacy. The implication for design is that boosting self-efficacy during onboarding can magnify the return on a strong selection decision.
  • Boundary conditions — predictor strength varies by job complexity. GMA's validity is highest in cognitively demanding roles (managerial, professional, technical) and lower in routine roles where conscientiousness alone is often the better single bet.
  • Incremental validity — adding a structured interview on top of a GMA test produces additional explained variance (it picks up traits the test does not), whereas adding another interview to an existing interview battery typically does not. The principle: combine different types of method, not multiple instances of the same type.
  • Adverse-impact awareness — cognitive-ability tests can show group-mean score differences across demographic groups; valid use therefore requires evidence the test predicts performance for all groups (no differential validity), and ideally pairing with non-cognitive predictors to mitigate impact while preserving validity.
  • Exam move — defend cognitive ability tests + conscientiousness over interviews and grades. Cite Cappelli (2019) to dismiss what most firms actually do, then propose a structured, multi-method battery anchored on these predictors.
Big Five / OCEAN Costa & McCrae · the five-factor model Personality
The five-factor personality model that underpins virtually every modern selection personality test — and the only personality dimension with strong, transportable predictive validity is conscientiousness.
  • O · Openness — curiosity, imagination, willingness to try new ideas; predicts adaptability to change and learning agility in novel roles.
  • C · Conscientiousness — discipline, organization, reliability, persistence. Across meta-analyses, the strongest personality predictor of job performance and the one most worth weighting in selection.
  • E · Extraversion — sociability, assertiveness, positive affect. Useful predictor for sales, management and team-leading roles; weak elsewhere.
  • A · Agreeableness — cooperation, trust, warmth. Predicts teamwork outcomes but can backfire in negotiation-heavy or adversarial roles.
  • N · Neuroticism (inverse: Emotional Stability) — anxiety, mood instability under pressure. High neuroticism reduces resilience; low neuroticism predicts calm performance in stressful jobs.
  • Use in selection — pair OCEAN with the Judge et al. hierarchy; conscientiousness as the headline weight, the other four as context-dependent. Always combine with a cognitive test, never use personality alone.
  • Application — Fuzzme! sim — the in-class simulation runs Big Five profiles against role requirements, illustrating both validity and the limits of personality-only selection.
  • Faking and social desirability — self-report personality tests can be gamed by candidates who infer the desired profile. Counter-measures: forced-choice formats, embedded validity scales, triangulation with structured interview behaviour, post-hire calibration.
  • Cross-cultural caveat — Big Five factor structure is robust across most national cultures, but interpretation of individual items (especially Extraversion and Agreeableness norms) varies enough that off-the-shelf norms should be checked locally.
  • Dark-side traits — beyond the Big Five, derailment research (Hogan, Kaiser) identifies counter-productive trait clusters that emerge under stress (e.g. arrogance, perfectionism, attention-seeking). Useful complement at executive levels where derailment cost is highest.
  • Trait–situation interaction — personality predicts behaviour more strongly in "weak" situations (low constraint, high discretion) and less strongly in "strong" situations (high structure, scripted role). Implications: weight personality more for autonomous roles, less for highly scripted ones.
  • Personality vs values — personality (OCEAN) is relatively stable; values are more malleable and shift with socialization. Use personality to predict how someone will work and values frameworks (Spencer Stuart) to predict whether they will fit the culture.
  • Exam move — when asked "design a selection test", name the Big Five, single out conscientiousness, and explicitly pair with a GMA test to avoid the personality-only trap.
Three Phases of Selection Fisher, Schoenfeldt & Shaw · 2014 Process audit
A phase model for executing and validating selection — and the cleanest diagnostic for figuring out where a broken hiring system actually breaks.
  • Phase 1 · Measurement — focuses on the accuracy and consistency of each technique. Are your tests valid? Reliable? Are interviewers scoring with a rubric, or eyeballing? Failure here means every later decision is based on noise.
  • Phase 1 diagnostics — high inter-rater disagreement, missing rubric, tests with no local validation evidence, mismatch between job analysis and what the interview actually probes.
  • Phase 2 diagnostics — opaque or shifting weighting, single decision-maker overriding panel scores, no documented decision rule, last-impression bias driving the call.
  • Phase 3 diagnostics — no tracking of new-hire performance against predictor scores, no monitoring of retention by hiring channel, no diversity audit of the funnel, no closed loop into the next hiring cycle.
  • Phase 2 · Decision-making — focuses on the completeness of candidate data and the rule used to combine it. Common failures: compensatory rules that let one strong score mask one critical weakness, or arbitrary cut-offs applied unevenly.
  • Phase 3 · Evaluation — focuses on the process from the organization's perspective: hire quality, time-to-fill, retention at 12/24 months, diversity outcomes, cost-per-hire. This is where selection meets HR analytics.
  • Why the model matters — most "our hiring is broken" complaints conflate the three phases; the fix differs sharply depending on whether the problem is measurement, decision logic, or organizational evaluation.
  • Symptom-to-phase mapping — high variance between interviewers? Phase 1. Strong on tests, weak on the job? Phase 2 decision rule weights are wrong. Hires retain at 6 months but quit by 18? Phase 3 (the org outcome) is the gap, often pointing back to onboarding rather than selection.
  • Linking to analytics — Phase 3 is where selection becomes a data problem: validity studies, correlations between predictor scores and 12/24-month performance, adverse-impact monitoring across protected characteristics.
  • Exam move — frame to audit a broken selection system: name the phase, name the symptom, propose the targeted fix (e.g. add a structured rubric for Phase 1; switch from compensatory to multiple-hurdle for Phase 2; introduce 12-month performance correlation studies for Phase 3).
Person–Organization vs Person–Job Fit Block 3 deck · classic selection theory Core trade-off
Two distinct selection criteria with very different downstream consequences — culture fit is socializable, skill fit usually isn't, and over-indexing on either is its own pathology.
  • P–O fit — alignment with the firm's culture, values and norms. Sources of evidence: values surveys, structured cultural interviews, simulations, the Spencer Stuart culture map.
  • P–J fit — alignment between the candidate's KSAOs (knowledge, skills, abilities, other characteristics) and the job's task requirements. Measured by ability tests, work samples, technical interviews.
  • Crucial asymmetry — P–O fit can be built through socialization (Three Domains); P–J fit on hard skills usually cannot be built fast enough to recover from a bad hire.
  • The diversity trap — over-selecting for P–O fit produces homogeneity, "culture clones", and reduced innovation. The famous airport-test interview question is the textbook bad practice.
  • Right mix — high P–J fit on the non-trainable skills (the things you can't socialize), enough P–O fit to function inside the firm's norms, room for cultural add rather than cultural match.
  • P–G fit (person-group) — sometimes treated as a third dimension: alignment with the immediate team's working style, complementary rather than identical skills, balance of cognitive styles. Often the proximal driver of new-hire experience.
  • P–V fit (person-vocation) — alignment with the broader profession or vocation; tends to be the most stable form of fit because it pre-dates any specific employer and is shaped by long-term interests and identity.
  • Faking culture-fit signals — sophisticated candidates can mirror cultural language back to interviewers; structured behavioural evidence and reference triangulation are the primary defences.
  • Lifecycle of fit — P–J fit matters most at hire and in the first year; P–O fit matters most for retention beyond year two. The two evolve at different speeds and through different mechanisms (performance feedback vs cultural socialization).
  • Measurement caveat — P–O fit assessments are notoriously easy to fake; structured behavioural interviews and reference triangulation help.
  • "Culture add" reframing — a growing body of practice replaces "culture fit" with "culture add", asking what the candidate brings that the existing culture lacks. This preserves the integration benefit while actively expanding diversity of thought.
  • Sequencing in the interview — assess P–J fit first (it's the harder gate); use P–O fit as a tie-breaker between technically strong candidates rather than a filter that screens technically strong but culturally unfamiliar candidates out early.
  • Exam move — defend the right mix; cite Spencer Stuart for measuring P–O fit, and call out the diversity cost of culture-fit maximalism. Bonus credit for naming "culture add" as the operationalized fix.

Spencer Stuart Culture Alignment Spencer Stuart · 8-style culture framework Culture map
An 8-style culture map plotted on two axes — the most operational tool for measuring P–O fit and for visualizing what a culture-change initiative is actually shifting.
  • Axis 1 · Independence ↔ Interdependence — how people relate to one another (autonomy-led vs collaboration-led).
  • Axis 2 · Flexibility ↔ Stability — how the organization responds to change (adaptive/exploratory vs predictable/standardized).
  • Measurement instrument — typically a forced-choice survey administered to a representative sample of employees and to candidates, with results aggregated to plot dominant and secondary styles.
  • Reading the map — every culture has a dominant style and a couple of secondary styles; the strongest cultures are coherent across axes, the weakest show contradictory styles at similar weights.
  • Aspirational vs actual — leaders often describe an aspirational culture while employees experience a different actual culture; the map exposes this gap and forces the conversation about which is being managed.
  • Designing onboarding from the map — once the target culture is plotted, onboarding content can be deliberately calibrated to transmit the dominant styles through rituals, stories and observed leadership behaviour.
  • Eight styles around the map — Learning (curiosity, exploration), Purpose (idealism, contribution), Caring (warmth, relationships), Order (rules, structure), Safety (planning, risk-aversion), Authority (decisiveness, hierarchy), Results (achievement, execution), Enjoyment (fun, spontaneity).
  • Use 1 — measure P–O fit — plot the candidate's preferred style and the organization's current style; the distance is a quantified fit score.
  • Use 2 — design culture change — define current vs target on the same map; any culture-change programme is a vector between two points on these axes.
  • Use 3 — diagnose dysfunction — strong Authority + strong Caring is unusual; strong Order + strong Learning creates tension. The map exposes contradictions leaders tolerate.
  • Cultural sub-units — large firms often show different dominant styles by function (Sales = Results + Enjoyment; Legal = Order + Safety; R&D = Learning + Purpose). The map quantifies the sub-cultural reality that single-firm "culture statements" tend to obscure.
  • Use 4 — M&A diligence — overlay the acquirer's and target's culture maps; the distance and the contradictions predict integration cost and post-merger attrition risk far better than financials alone.
  • Limits — the eight-style typology is a simplification; cultures evolve faster than annual surveys can track; and self-report carries social-desirability bias. Triangulate with behavioural artefacts (meeting norms, decision processes, reward systems).
  • Exam move — plot candidate vs org to measure P–O fit; or redesign culture by naming which axis the firm needs to move along.
Structured vs Unstructured Interviews Armstrong · meta-analytic verdict Method validity
The validity verdict from decades of meta-analyses — and the single highest-leverage change most firms could make to their hiring.
  • Structured interviews — same questions for every candidate, scored against a rubric, ideally panel-based, with answers compared before discussion. Highest validity of any interview format and roughly on par with cognitive testing for many roles.
  • Unstructured interviews — free-flowing conversations, interviewer's choice of questions, holistic impression. Cappelli (2019): poor predictor of subsequent job performance, dominant in practice, defended on grounds of "chemistry" or "fit".
  • Why structure works — (i) it forces comparability across candidates, (ii) it surfaces bias to peer review, (iii) the rubric anchors decisions in evidence rather than impressions.
  • Common objections (and refutations) — "robotic" (well-designed structured interviews still allow follow-ups); "candidates resent it" (data does not show this when explained); "we lose the gut signal" (the gut signal is the bias).
  • Design checklist — job-analysis-derived questions, behavioural or situational format, behaviourally anchored rating scale, panel of trained interviewers, independent scoring before deliberation.
  • Validity gain in numbers — meta-analyses typically place structured interview validity around r ≈ 0.50, unstructured around r ≈ 0.20; converting an unstructured interview into a structured one is among the highest-ROI interventions available to an HR function.
  • Implementation friction — the cost is interviewer training, rubric design, and discipline to score before deliberating. Firms that pilot the change typically see resistance from senior interviewers who value their "feel" — exactly the bias the structure is designed to neutralize.
  • Mini-rubric design — three to five competencies derived from the job analysis, each scored 1–5 with anchored descriptors (1 = "no evidence", 3 = "meets bar", 5 = "outstanding evidence"). Anchors must be behavioural, not adjectival.
  • Panel logistics — 2–4 trained interviewers, divided question domains so coverage is full, parallel scoring sheets, a brief calibration discussion after independent scoring is recorded.
  • Candidate experience — explained structure raises perceived fairness; surveys consistently show candidates prefer structured interviews when the rationale is communicated up front, dispelling the "robotic" objection.
  • Exam move — defend a structured + panel + scoring rubric set-up; quote Cappelli on unstructured interviews; pair with the predictor hierarchy and quote the validity-coefficient delta if you can.
Situational vs Behavioural Questions Block 3 deck · structured interview typology Question design
The two flavours of structured question — and the experience-level rule for choosing which to use.
  • Situational — hypothetical scenarios: "What would you do if a major client threatened to leave on your second week?" Works for candidates with little or no relevant experience; surfaces reasoning, judgement and values.
  • Behavioural — past behaviour: "Tell me about a time you turned around an unhappy client." Works best for experienced candidates; surfaces what they have actually done, not what they say they would do.
  • Underlying principle — past behaviour is the best predictor of future behaviour when there is enough past behaviour to ask about. For graduates and career-switchers, lean situational.
  • Situational question design — describe a realistic, role-relevant scenario; ask what the candidate would do, why, and what alternatives they considered; score against a behaviourally anchored rubric ("ideal answer", "acceptable", "weak").
  • Behavioural question design — open with "Tell me about a time when…" anchored to a competency from the job analysis; allow the candidate to choose the example (rather than asking for a specific one); probe systematically for situation, task, action and result.
  • Mix in one interview — a strong structured interview typically blends both, anchored to the same job-analysis competencies and scored on the same rubric.
  • Follow-up probes — for behavioural questions, probe for the specific action and the specific result; for situational questions, probe for the reasoning chain and the alternatives considered. Probes do not break the structure as long as the scoring criteria remain fixed.
  • Bad questions to avoid — leading questions ("you would obviously…"), brainteasers without job relevance, hypotheticals about protected characteristics. All three reduce validity and increase legal risk.
  • Exam move — match question type to candidate experience level; argue that mixing both gives the richest evidence per minute of interview time.
STAR Method & the 4 P's Behavioural-interview answer formula Candidate toolkit
The candidate-side companion to behavioural interviewing — a four-part answer frame plus a four-part preparation routine.
  • S · Situation — set the scene briefly: where, when, who. Two sentences maximum; do not burn the answer on context.
  • T · Task — define the specific challenge or objective you faced. Make the stakes explicit.
  • A · Action — your specific actions, not "we". This is where most answers fail: candidates default to collective pronouns and obscure their own contribution.
  • R · Result — quantifiable outcome plus what you learned. Numbers beat adjectives.
  • 4 P's preparation routine — Polish your résumé, Prepare 6–10 STAR stories mapped to common competencies, Prepare 3–5 questions for the interviewer, Practice aloud.
  • Why behavioural answers must be personal — interviewers are scoring you, not your team; the rubric expects ownership of specific actions, not narration of group activity.
  • Story portfolio mapping — strong candidates prepare a small portfolio of stories (typically 6–10) and tag each against multiple competencies (leadership, conflict resolution, analytical thinking, customer focus), so the same story can be re-aimed at different questions.
  • Common STAR failure modes — over-long Situation/Task, vague Actions, missing or unquantified Result, no learning reflection. A clean STAR answer should run 90–120 seconds.
  • Reverse use — interviewer side — interviewers can use STAR as a scoring frame, awarding points per element. This forces consistency across candidates and makes panel deliberations evidence-based.
  • CAR / PAR variants — Context-Action-Result and Problem-Action-Result are widely used in CV writing; STAR is preferred for spoken interview answers because the Task element forces explicit framing of stakes before describing actions.
  • "We" stories — when the only honest answer involves group action (e.g. team projects), candidates should still narrate their specific contribution explicitly: "the team agreed to X; my role was to Y; I designed and ran Z." Even in collective contexts, the rubric is scoring the individual.
  • Negative stories — interviewers increasingly ask for a time something went wrong. Strong STAR answers here name the failure, take ownership of the specific action that contributed, and end with what changed in their practice as a result — not with a "humble brag" that wasn't really a failure.
  • Practice cadence — out-loud rehearsal of full STAR answers, ideally with a peer who interrupts with probes, is dramatically more effective than silent re-reading. The 4 P's' "Practice" step is the most-skipped and the highest-return preparation activity.
  • Exam move — defend why behavioural answers must be specific, personal and measurable; use STAR to explain what makes a structured interview scorable.
Validity & Reliability of Tests Armstrong · psychometric foundations Test quality
The two psychometric requirements that any selection instrument — interview, test or simulation — has to clear before its scores mean anything.
  • Validity — does the instrument predict the outcome it claims to? Sub-types: content validity (does it cover the job?), criterion validity (does it correlate with performance?), construct validity (does it measure the underlying trait?).
  • Reliability — does the instrument give consistent results across raters, time, and forms? An interview rated 7/10 by one panellist and 3/10 by another fails reliability and therefore can't be valid.
  • Logical order — reliability is a necessary but not sufficient condition for validity. A test can be reliably wrong; a valid test must first be reliable.
  • Three common test types — intelligence/cognitive ability tests, personality tests (typically OCEAN-based), and aptitude tests (numerical, verbal, mechanical, spatial).
  • Defensibility — selection decisions challenged on discrimination grounds usually fail at validity or reliability documentation; keep audit trails.
  • Inter-rater reliability — for any interview or assessor exercise, calibrate scorers before live use and re-calibrate periodically. Cohen's kappa or intraclass correlation coefficients are the standard metrics.
  • Test-retest reliability — administer the same test twice across a short interval; expect high but not perfect correlation. Personality scales typically test-retest above 0.80; ability tests higher.
  • Local validation — published validity coefficients are general; running a local validation study (predictor scores vs subsequent performance ratings in your own firm) is the gold standard and the strongest defence against legal challenge.
  • Face validity — a softer concept: does the test look relevant to the candidate? Important for candidate experience and acceptance, but not a substitute for criterion validity. A test can have low face validity and high criterion validity (e.g. cognitive ability), or vice versa (e.g. graphology).
  • Generalizability — validity established in one job family or culture may not transfer; transporting a US-validated test into a new geography without re-validation is a common source of selection error.
  • Exam move — defend dropping a popular-but-invalid test; argue that adding any new instrument requires evidence of both validity and reliability, ideally locally validated.
Job Description vs Job Specification Armstrong · the recruiting starting point Foundation pair
The two documents you cannot recruit without — the same pairing as in Block 2, now applied as the input to every selection decision.
  • Job description — duties, responsibilities, working environment, reporting relationships, key deliverables, working conditions. Tells the candidate (and the recruiter) what the role does.
  • Job specification (person spec) — knowledge, skills, abilities and other characteristics (KSAOs), required qualifications, experience, traits. Tells the recruiter what the role requires.
  • Why both — the description grounds the advert and onboarding; the specification grounds the scoring rubric, the test selection, and any later capability framework.
  • Essential vs desirable — every line on the spec should be tagged; over-listing essentials shrinks the funnel and concentrates bias.
  • Refresh cadence — both documents should be refreshed every time the role re-opens, not copied from a four-year-old template.
  • Source for both — derived from job analysis (Block 2): observation, interviews with current job-holders, work-sample analysis, task lists. Without a fresh job analysis, the documents drift toward what the previous job-holder did rather than what the role currently requires.
  • Downstream uses — the description anchors the offer letter and onboarding plan; the specification anchors the interview scoring rubric, the test selection, and the future performance-review competencies.
  • Inclusion design — gendered language audits, requirement minimalism (no degree if not needed), explicit flexibility statements all materially widen the applicant pool. The description is a recruitment marketing artefact, not just an internal document.
  • Sign-off path — both documents should be jointly approved by HR, the hiring manager, and (where relevant) the function head. Lack of joint sign-off is a leading cause of "we hired the wrong person" post-mortems.
  • Exam move — note that without both documents, recruitment has no defensible criteria, and that out-of-date specs are a frequent root cause of "shortage" problems.
Internal vs External Recruitment Sources Armstrong · sourcing trade-off Build vs buy
Two source families with different costs, signals and unintended consequences — the canonical "build vs buy" of talent.
  • Internal sources — promotions, transfers, internal job boards, referrals from existing employees. Pros: lower cost, faster onboarding, known performance history, culturally aligned, motivating signal that growth is possible. Cons: opens a downstream vacancy (the "domino effect"), can entrench existing biases, limits fresh ideas.
  • External sources — job ads, LinkedIn and other social platforms, agencies, head-hunters, university milk rounds, career fairs. Pros: fresh skills, new perspectives, expanded diversity pool, signals openness. Cons: costlier per hire, longer time-to-productivity, no track record, weaker culture fit signal.
  • Strategic rule — internal-first is consistent with a "build" talent strategy; external is consistent with a "buy" strategy and is dominant at senior/specialist levels where the firm lacks the bench.
  • Referrals — the hybrid — referrals combine external candidate with internal endorsement; high quality, fast, but a known driver of demographic homogeneity if not actively managed.
  • Mix shifts by role level — entry-level roles tilt external (graduate intakes); middle management tilts internal (promotions); executive roles tilt heavily external via search firms, where the cost of search is justified by the strategic stakes.
  • Speed-vs-quality trade — internal sources fill faster and cheaper; external sources fill slower but bring breadth. Time-to-fill pressure systematically biases choices toward internal even when external would be more strategic.
  • Boomerang hires — former employees re-hired; combine external-source attributes (fresh perspectives from elsewhere) with internal-source attributes (known performance, faster onboarding). Increasingly common; require clean exit policies to keep the door open.
  • Contingent workforce — freelancers, contractors and gig workers form an external source with different selection logic (short-cycle work samples, portfolio review, references from prior engagements rather than employers).
  • Talent communities — building a passive pipeline of pre-qualified candidates (newsletter subscribers, alumni groups, past finalists) lowers time-to-fill at the next opening and softens the build-vs-buy choice into a continuum.
  • Channel attribution — every funnel measurement should tag the source channel so quality-of-hire can eventually be compared across channels. Most firms collect this data and never analyse it.
  • Exam move — choose by strategy (build-vs-buy) and position level; flag the domino-effect risk for internal and the homogeneity risk for referral-heavy pipelines.
Employer Brand & EVP Armstrong · "employer of choice" framing Attraction
Recruitment is a two-way market — candidates are buyers as much as sellers, and the firm's brand determines who applies in the first place.
  • Employer brand — the external reputation of the firm as an employer. Built through current and former employees, Glassdoor and review platforms, public communications, observable behaviour during recruitment.
  • EVP (Employee Value Proposition) — the bundle of tangible and intangible rewards on offer: compensation, benefits, career development, culture, mission, work-life balance, manager quality. The promise the firm is making.
  • Segmenting the EVP — different talent segments value different EVP components (early-career: learning + brand; mid-career: career mobility + flexibility; senior: impact + autonomy + total reward). One-size EVP messaging under-converts on every segment.
  • The hidden EVP cost — every job ad and recruitment touchpoint promises something; firms routinely under-track whether the promise was kept. Engagement surveys at six and twelve months provide the missing closed-loop signal.
  • Two-way market — strong candidates are simultaneously evaluated and evaluating; weak EVP narrows the applicant pool, biases self-selection, and forces over-payment to compensate.
  • Coherence test — the EVP shown in recruitment must match the experience of the first 90 days; mismatched expectations are one of Armstrong's named recruitment problems and a top driver of early attrition.
  • Brand as filter — clear EVP attracts self-selecting candidates whose values align, raising P–O fit before the interview even starts.
  • Glassdoor and reverse signalling — public review platforms have shifted EVP from a controlled message to a triangulated signal; firms that ignore review-platform feedback effectively concede the narrative to ex-employees.
  • EVP components to articulate — total reward, career growth, learning opportunities, manager quality, work-life flexibility, culture and values, mission/impact, brand prestige. Naming which of these the firm leads on, and which it concedes, sharpens the message.
  • Internal brand — current employees are the most credible brand ambassadors; investment in employee experience pays back in referral quality and external reputation simultaneously.
  • Leader visibility — senior leaders' public communications (LinkedIn, podcasts, conference talks) are part of the employer brand whether or not HR coordinates them. Aligned executive presence amplifies EVP; misaligned presence undermines it.
  • Brand-EVP alignment audit — periodic check: does what the firm says in recruitment match what new hires experience in their first 90 days? Mismatches detected here predict early attrition months in advance.
  • Exam move — show that recruitment competitiveness depends on brand; link brand strength to shortage problems and to the cost side of the recruitment funnel.
Case spotlight

The "great culture fit" hire who never produced

A consulting firm hires a graduate who scores top-quartile on a values-fit interview, charms the partners, and is offered a role over a more technically capable candidate. Twelve months in, performance reviews are weak. The firm calls it a "selection failure" — but the diagnosis is more interesting.

Read against Block 3: the firm over-weighted P–O fit and under-tested P–J fit; the interview was unstructured (Cappelli's exact warning); no cognitive-ability test was administered (Judge et al.'s strongest predictor was skipped); and the onboarding skipped the task domain of socialization, so role expectations were never made explicit. Four frameworks, one bad hire — and the fix is structural, not personal.

Three Domains of Socialization Block 3 deck · onboarding theory Onboarding core
Onboarding doesn't happen in one channel — it happens in three concentric layers, and skipping any of them breaks integration in a predictable way.
  • Layer 1 · Organization — values, mission, history, formal policies, code of conduct, HR systems, broader strategy. Tells the new hire what the firm is.
  • Layer 2 · Group / Department — supervisor expectations, team norms, peer relationships, departmental rituals, who-knows-what, team expertise. Tells the new hire how this team works.
  • Layer 3 · Task — specific responsibilities, performance expectations, deadlines, resources, tools, systems, access. Tells the new hire what to do tomorrow morning.
  • Pre-boarding — increasingly recognized as a fourth, earlier layer: the period between offer-acceptance and start date. Used well, it surfaces logistics (equipment, paperwork, introductions) and primes the organization domain before day one.
  • Symptom signatures — high early voluntary attrition usually signals task-domain failure (role clarity, expectations). Long-tail under-performance often signals group-domain failure (no peer integration, weak supervisor relationship). Cultural complaints six months in usually signal organization-domain failure.
  • Domain interactions — the layers reinforce one another: a clear task brief (task domain) is more meaningful when the new hire understands strategic context (organization domain) and trusts peers enough to ask questions (group domain). Strong onboarding works on all three simultaneously, not sequentially in isolation.
  • Remote / hybrid amplification — distributed work amplifies group- and task-domain risk: informal water-cooler scaffolding disappears, supervisor visibility drops, role clarity has to be made explicit through documents that would otherwise emerge through proximity.
  • Most-skipped layer — task. Firms over-invest in orientation week (organization) and under-invest in "what does done look like in week three?" (task).
  • Diagnostic value — when a new hire flounders, ask which domain was thin. Org-skip produces values misalignment; group-skip produces isolation; task-skip produces role-clarity collapse and the early-attrition spike.
  • Pairs with — Socialization Strategies (sequential / individual / collective) for how each domain is delivered.
  • Time horizon — organization-level socialization typically spans the first 30 days, group-level extends through 60–90 days, and task-level continues to the first formal performance review. A 90-day check-in that touches all three domains catches gaps before they become resignation triggers.
  • Who owns what — HR usually owns the organization domain (orientation, policies, systems), the line manager owns the group and task domains (norms, expectations, tools), and peers own the informal scaffolding inside the group domain (who-knows-what, "this is how we really do it").
  • Exam move — diagnose any onboarding failure by which layer was skipped, then prescribe a domain-specific fix with an explicit owner and time horizon.
Socialization Strategies Block 3 deck · onboarding delivery typology Delivery design
A short typology of how onboarding is delivered — sequencing, granularity, and group composition each shift the experience and the outcomes.
  • Sequential — predetermined timing, stages and duration. Day 1: orientation. Week 1: systems. Week 2: shadow. Useful for high-volume roles and regulated environments; can feel rigid for senior or autonomous hires.
  • Individual — one-to-one, typically supervisor or buddy paired with the new hire. Tailored, high-touch, fits non-standard or senior roles; but quality varies sharply with the supervisor.
  • Collective — group cohort training at the start, often with a shared start date. Builds cohort bonds, accelerates peer networks, reinforces shared culture; classic in graduate programmes and consulting firm intakes.
  • Combinations — most modern programmes layer all three: collective orientation week (collective + sequential) + ongoing buddy system (individual).
  • Investiture vs divestiture — orthogonal dimension: investiture programmes affirm the new hire's existing identity and skills (typical for experienced hires); divestiture programmes deliberately reshape identity through immersion (typical for military, professional services bootcamps, religious orders).
  • Formal vs informal — formal onboarding has explicit, documented steps; informal happens through everyday peer interaction. Strong programmes design both, knowing that informal carries most of the cultural transmission.
  • Serial vs disjunctive — serial socialization pairs the new hire with an experienced role-model; disjunctive leaves the new hire to invent the role. Serial accelerates integration but can entrench existing practice; disjunctive allows innovation at the cost of higher early risk.
  • Programme cost vs depth — collective + sequential is the most cost-efficient per head; individual is the most expensive but the highest-fidelity. Match cost to strategic importance of the role.
  • Fixed vs variable timing — fixed-timing programmes (everyone hits the same milestones on the same calendar) are easier to manage; variable-timing programmes (milestones triggered by individual readiness) produce better learning but heavier coordination overhead.
  • Mentorship as connective tissue — pairing every new hire with a mentor outside the immediate reporting line extends the group domain beyond the team and accelerates organization-domain learning through trusted relay.
  • Trade-offs — collective builds cohort bonds and cost efficiency; individual gives tailored attention and faster role-specific learning; sequential gives auditability and equality of experience.
  • Tracking and accountability — sequential strategies are the easiest to audit (you can confirm what was delivered when); individual strategies need a checklist or buddy charter to avoid drift; collective strategies need a measured handoff into individual coaching so cohort momentum doesn't die when the programme ends.
  • Exam move — match the delivery strategy to the role's autonomy level and the volume of the intake; argue that good onboarding usually blends all three rather than choosing one, and name which strategy delivers each of the three socialization domains.

Training Process Model Block 3 deck · the four-part learning cycle Training core
A four-step cyclical model that frames every effective training programme — skip the first step and you design for the wrong gap; skip the last and you can't prove the programme worked.
  • Step 1 · Needs assessment — three lenses: organizational (does strategy require a new capability?), task (which tasks need new skills?), person (which people lack them?). Skipping this step is the single most common reason training "doesn't work".
  • Step 2 · Design — write learning objectives, draft lesson plans, sequence content, choose assessment methods, define success criteria. Objectives must be observable and measurable.
  • Step 3 · Implementation — pick the method that fits the content: classroom, on-the-job, e-learning, simulation, coaching, action learning, blended. Each has a cost / fidelity / scalability profile.
  • Step 4 · Evaluation — measure at four levels (Kirkpatrick); feed results back into Needs Assessment for the next cycle. The arrow loops; this is a cycle, not a line.
  • Needs-analysis tools — organizational lens: strategy maps, capability gap analysis. Task lens: job analysis, competency frameworks, work-sample reviews. Person lens: performance reviews, 360 feedback, self-assessment, skill tests.
  • Design heuristics — chunk content into 30–45 minute segments; alternate concept exposition with deliberate practice; build in immediate feedback; finish each segment with a transfer-of-learning commitment (what will you try this week?).
  • Failure-mode diagnostics — content irrelevant? Step 1 was skipped. Learners confused? Step 2 was rushed. Low engagement? Step 3 chose the wrong method. Can't prove ROI? Step 4 stayed at Level 1.
  • Method-selection grid — classroom for conceptual content with low practice need; on-the-job for procedural skills with high context-specificity; e-learning for scalable, standardized knowledge; simulation for high-stakes or low-frequency tasks (negotiation, safety, crisis response); coaching for behavioural change at the individual level. Most programmes blend two or three.
  • Spaced practice — distributing learning across multiple sessions outperforms massed practice for retention; modern programmes therefore schedule reinforcement modules at 7, 30 and 90 days rather than running a single intensive course.
  • Manager involvement — programmes in which the line manager pre-briefs the learner, observes application, and debriefs significantly out-perform programmes where the manager is absent from the design. Transfer of training is overwhelmingly a manager-quality variable.
  • Transfer of training — the gap between what is learned in training and what is applied on the job. Strengthened by manager reinforcement, opportunity to practice immediately, and explicit Level-3 expectations set at design time.
  • Exam move — diagnose why a training programme failed by naming the missed step; structure any "design a training programme" answer around the four steps, and add the method-selection logic to score above a recital.
Kirkpatrick Training Evaluation Levels Donald Kirkpatrick · four-level evaluation model Evaluation
The four-level model that operationalizes Step 4 of the Training Process Model — and the rhetorical lever for arguing past "happy-sheet" evaluations.
  • Level 1 · Reactions — did participants like the training? Measured via satisfaction surveys ("happy sheets") and Net Promoter-style scores. Necessary but trivial; high reactions are not the same as learning.
  • Level 2 · Learning — did knowledge or skill change? Measured by pre/post tests, role-plays, certification exams. The first level that gives evidence the content actually transferred.
  • Level 3 · Behavior — did learners apply the new behaviour on the job? Measured 60–90 days after training via manager observation, 360 feedback, on-the-job assessment. Where most programmes fail — content is learned but never used.
  • Level 4 · Results — did the firm-level outcome move? Measured by KPIs, productivity, quality, customer satisfaction, sales, retention; ideally with a control group or a difference-in-differences design.
  • Phillips' Level 5 extension — some practitioners add a fifth level for explicit ROI calculation (financial benefits minus programme costs, expressed as a percentage). Useful for senior-leader-facing programmes; overkill for most.
  • Measurement timing — Level 1 immediately post-session; Level 2 at end of programme; Level 3 at 60–90 days; Level 4 at 6–12 months. Trying to compress this timeline produces noisy or premature signals.
  • Linking to performance management — Level 3 measurement effectively requires manager observation, which is also the substrate of the Block 4 performance management cycle. Designing the two systems together avoids duplicate measurement burden.
  • Leading vs lagging indicators — Levels 1–2 are leading (predict whether change is likely); Levels 3–4 are lagging (confirm whether change actually happened). A balanced dashboard uses both rather than waiting for lag measures alone.
  • Diminishing measurement — each level is harder and more expensive to measure than the last, which is why most firms stop at Level 1 — and why most can't defend their training budget.
  • Linking to ROI — Level 4 enables cost-benefit analysis: programme cost vs measured business impact. This is the language the CFO understands.
  • Pragmatic application — evaluate all programmes at Level 1; sample at Level 2; pick a small number of strategic programmes for Levels 3 and 4. Trying to do every level on every programme is what kills evaluation in practice.
  • Common critique — Kirkpatrick is causal in its language but correlational in practice; Level 4 outcomes are confounded by everything else happening in the firm. The remedy is design (control groups, staggered rollouts) rather than abandoning the framework.
  • Exam move — push past Level 1 reactions to prove training works; argue that "we collect feedback forms" is not evaluation, and propose a sampled Level 3 / Level 4 design for strategically important programmes.
Training vs Development Block 3 deck · scope of learning interventions Conceptual split
The conceptual split between learning for the present job and learning for a future role — both are needed, and confusing the two is a budgeting trap.
  • Training — KSAs for the current job. Tightly scoped, often technical or procedural, evaluated against current-role performance. Examples: software upskilling, compliance training, sales-process certification.
  • Development — preparation for future, broader or higher-level roles. Loosely scoped, more strategic and behavioural, evaluated against career progression and successor readiness. Examples: leadership programmes, stretch assignments, MBA sponsorship, coaching.
  • Strategic implication — a firm that only trains will operate today but starve its succession pipeline; a firm that only develops will create frustrated learners who lack current-job skills.
  • Budgeting trap — when costs are tight, development is cut first because returns are slower and harder to attribute. The medium-term cost is a thinner internal-recruitment bench (back to Internal vs External).
  • Delivery — training often classroom or e-learning; development typically through assignments, mentoring, coaching, exposure to senior leaders, formal programmes.
  • 70-20-10 heuristic — a long-standing practitioner rule that development comes roughly 70% from on-the-job stretch assignments, 20% from coaching and relationships, and 10% from formal courses. The numbers are heuristic, not empirical, but the message — that development is mostly built through deliberate experience — is robust.
  • Linking back to selection — a strong development pipeline is the precondition for internal recruitment (Block 3) and for succession planning (Block 2 workforce planning). Cutting development is therefore a hidden cost to future selection.
  • High-potential (HiPo) programmes — a specific development sub-stream targeted at identified successors; combines stretch assignments, executive coaching, exposure to senior leaders, and formal learning. The classic case where development spend is justified by named individuals rather than by aggregate ROI.
  • Self-directed learning — modern learning platforms (LinkedIn Learning, Coursera, internal LXPs) shift part of the development burden to the individual. Useful for breadth and currency, weak on behavioural change — still requires coaching and managerial reinforcement to translate to performance.
  • Career conversations — structured periodic discussions between employee and manager covering aspirations, capability gaps, possible next moves, and development actions. The least costly developmental intervention with one of the largest engagement returns.
  • Job rotation and stretch assignments — the most concrete development levers; pair a high-potential employee with a temporary role outside their comfort zone, with explicit learning objectives and a debrief. Cheap relative to formal programmes, high in transfer.
  • Exam move — scope a learning plan by separating training from development; argue for both, link development to the firm's "build" talent strategy, and explicitly connect the development budget to the internal-recruitment pipeline.

Pulling it together — the Block 3 logic in one paragraph

Start with Armstrong's eight-step arc as the structural spine. Inside Step 1, write a job description and a person specification, and use Spencer Stuart's 8-style map to articulate the cultural requirement; this prevents the recruitment criteria from being implicit. In Steps 2–3, manage the trade-off between internal and external sources and protect the funnel with a credible employer brand and EVP — recruitment is a two-way market and the firm is also being interviewed. In Step 4, weight the selection battery according to Judge et al.: cognitive ability and conscientiousness first, structured behavioural interview using STAR-scoreable questions second, work samples and Big Five context-dependent thereafter; every instrument must clear validity and reliability. In Step 5, choose a decision rule that combines scores transparently, and audit the whole flow with Fisher, Schoenfeldt & Shaw's three phases. Once the offer is signed, hand off into the Three Domains of Socialization — organization, group and task — delivered through some combination of sequential, individual and collective strategies. From there, the loop continues into training (current job) and development (future role) on the four-step Training Process Model, with Kirkpatrick's four levels proving the programme worked and feeding the next round of needs assessment. The exam-essay payoff is that every framework in this chapter sits at a specific point on this loop; mention the loop, place the framework, defend the choice with the relevant empirical reference (Judge et al., Cappelli, Armstrong), and you are arguing like an HR economist rather than reciting a glossary.

Common traps the exam will test for

Most Block 3 exam questions present a vignette in which a firm has done something seemingly reasonable that the literature actually condemns. The high-scoring move is to spot the trap, name the framework that condemns it, and propose a clean fix. The four most common traps:

  • Trap 1 · "We hire on culture fit." Sounds modern; produces homogeneity and weak P–J signal. Fix: lead with structured P–J assessment (KSAOs, work samples), use P–O fit as a tie-breaker, and reframe as "culture add".
  • Trap 2 · "We rely on interviews and gut." Sounds professional; Cappelli's evidence says it's the weakest defensible combination. Fix: add a validated cognitive-ability test, structure the interview with a behaviourally anchored rubric, score independently before deliberating.
  • Trap 3 · "We measure training with smile sheets." Sounds rigorous; only reaches Kirkpatrick Level 1. Fix: sample Level 2 (pre/post tests) on every programme, design Level 3 (behaviour observation at 60–90 days) into strategic programmes, and reserve Level 4 (results) for the few programmes large enough to warrant a controlled study.
  • Trap 4 · "We threw them in the deep end." Sounds character-building; usually means the task domain of socialization was skipped and onboarding ended with the orientation deck. Fix: explicit task-level onboarding owned by the line manager, 30/60/90 check-ins, and a buddy in the same role family.

Exam moves — the high-leverage plays

  • Open any selection question with the predictor hierarchy (Judge et al.) + Cappelli's verdict on unstructured interviews. It's the easiest way to look well-read in one paragraph.
  • Use P–O vs P–J fit to argue for both skill and diversity — over-selecting on culture fit produces homogeneous teams and reduces innovation.
  • Cite Kirkpatrick to push training evaluation past Level 1 — satisfaction surveys are not evaluation; pair with the Training Process Model loop.
  • Diagnose onboarding failures by which of the three domains (organization / group / task) got skipped, then prescribe a domain-specific fix.
  • Audit a broken selection system by walking through Fisher, Schoenfeldt & Shaw's three phases — measurement, decision, evaluation — and naming the phase that's failing.
  • Defend structure in interviews with same questions + rubric + panel + independent scoring; use Cappelli to demolish the "we go on chemistry" defence.
  • Plot culture on the Spencer Stuart 8-style map to measure P–O fit quantitatively and to name what a culture-change initiative is actually shifting.
  • Separate training from development in any learning-design answer; defend the development budget by linking it to the internal-recruitment pipeline.
  • Use STAR + 4 P's if asked to coach a candidate; emphasize personal "I" actions and quantified results.
  • Map every framework onto Armstrong's eight-step arc — it converts a list of frameworks into a coherent process narrative and is the single best exam structure.
  • Pair every prescription with an empirical citation — Judge et al. for predictor weighting, Cappelli for the indictment of unstructured interviews, Armstrong for process and brand, Fisher/Schoenfeldt/Shaw for selection audit, Kirkpatrick for evaluation. Citations are the difference between an opinion and an argument.
  • Watch for the "selection failure" misdiagnosis — what looks like a hiring miss is often an onboarding miss; before redesigning selection, audit the Three Domains for the most recent under-performing hires.
  • Use the loop to argue for budget — evaluation (Kirkpatrick) feeds needs assessment, which justifies design, which determines implementation. Cut evaluation and the loop breaks; cut development and the internal pipeline starves; cut onboarding and selection ROI evaporates. Frame budget defences as protecting the loop.

Block 3 ends with a hand-off into Block 4 · Performance Management. Once selection, socialization and training have done their work, the next loop is about measuring and developing the performance you set up here. The Training Process Model already feeds Block 4 through its evaluation step; Kirkpatrick's Level 3 measurement is in effect a performance review of the trained behaviour. Carry these frameworks forward — they are the same logic seen from a different point in the employee lifecycle.

Chapter four · Block 4 · expanded

04Performance management & appraisal

Once people are in their roles, how do you tell them how they're doing — and what good looks like? Aguinis' framing is the cite that powers the whole block, and the design choices below are the levers every PM redesign turns.

Block 4's central debate is sharper than its title: most firms do appraisal (once a year, HR-driven, past-focused) and call it management. The interesting design choices live in how you measure (BARS, BOS, MBO/SMART, 360, forced distribution), against what (traits, behaviors, outcomes), against whom (absolute vs relative judgment), and by whom (manager, peer, 360). Layered on top are the rater-error problems (halo, leniency, recency...) that subjective ratings inherit by default — and the measurement-quality lens (relevance · deficiency · contamination) that audits whether the metric even captures performance in the first place.

The block is anchored by three named cases. Vitality Health is the canonical "PM redesign" case — moving from 13 absolute rating levels to a forced-distribution + competencies + SMART system. Alex Sander is the cautionary 360-feedback story — multi-source feedback misfires when the culture isn't ready. Deloitte is the practitioner reinvention — kill the ratings, run weekly check-ins, ask the team leader four future-focused questions. Together they show that every dial on the system (rating format, source, frequency, comparison logic) is a strategic choice — not an HR housekeeping detail.

The frameworks worth knowing cold

Performance Management Cycle — Identify · Measure · Develop Aguinis et al. (2009) · S11–S14 Core
The organizing spine of Block 4. PM is a continuous process — not an annual form — that aligns individual performance with strategy via three pillars and feeds both administrative and developmental uses.
  • Definition — PM is "a continuous process of identifying, measuring and developing the performance of individuals and teams, and aligning it with the strategic goals of the organization"
  • MOA logic — performance = Motivation × Opportunity × Ability. Each leg has to be present; PM diagnoses which leg is missing
  • Two uses — administrative (pay, promotion, layoffs) vs developmental (coaching, growth). Most systems try to do both — and end up serving neither
  • Move — frame any PM design question on this spine. "What is the system trying to identify? How is it measuring it? How does it develop the person from there?"
Appraisal vs Management — the Aguinis dial Aguinis, Joo & Gottfredson (2011) · "Why we hate PM" Core
Two ends of one dial. Most firms sit at the appraisal end and wonder why nobody likes the system. The fix is not a better form — it's sliding toward the management end.
  • The diagnosis — "we hate PM" usually means "we hate appraisal pretending to be management". The form is annual; the conversation is past-tense; the line manager outsources judgment to HR
  • Why it persists — appraisal is auditable (date, signature, rating) while management is hard to standardize. Compliance crowds out coaching
  • The fix — push the system toward the right pole: weekly check-ins, future-focused questions, line-manager ownership, less paperwork. Deloitte is the canonical example
  • Move — whenever a case asks "why is the PM system failing?", diagnose which end of the dial it sits on. Then prescribe the slide
Forced Distribution — the Vitality Health move Bingham & Beer · case 913-501 · S12 Core
Force raters to allocate employees into a fixed-percentage bell curve. Fixes rating inflation, breaks team cohesion. Use with care — it is the most political dial in PM.
  • Fixes — eliminates leniency & rating inflation; forces real differentiation; makes the high/low tails visible to compensation and succession
  • Risks — destroys team cohesion; can be deeply unfair in a high-talent team (someone has to be labelled "low" even if nobody is); breeds zero-sum politics among peers
  • Vitality categories — Top Achiever / Achiever / Low Achiever / Unacceptable / Not Rated
  • Move — defend or attack based on (a) culture, (b) maturity of raters, (c) team-level effects. Don't argue it system-wide if the unit of analysis is a 6-person team
Vitality Health Revised PM — the canonical redesign Bingham & Beer · case 913-501 Case
The case is the exam's go-to "PM redesign" reference. The shift is from 13 absolute rating levels (which inflated to meaninglessness) to forced-distribution + competencies + SMART + a common cycle + a richer incentive mix.
  • Diagnosis of the old system — 13 levels gave managers too much room to be polite; ratings drifted up; differentiation disappeared; compensation could not reward stars
  • Redesign elements — (1) forced-distribution categories, (2) job-specific competency lists, (3) SMART objectives, (4) standardised January cycle, (5) revised cash + equity incentive mix
  • Tensions left open — team-level fairness; reactions of solid-but-not-top performers reclassified as "Low"; manager training to defend categories
  • Move — cite as the canonical case for any "PM redesign" or "rating inflation" question. Always pair the rating change with the incentive change — they reinforce each other
360-Degree Feedback — multi-source Alex Sander case · Aguinis Core
Feedback from supervisor, peers, direct reports, and sometimes customers. Richer picture, surfaces blind spots, signals fairness. Lethal in low-trust cultures or when used administratively before the org is ready.
  • Strengths — captures the multiple "performances" a job actually has (delivery vs collaboration vs leadership); surfaces blind spots a single supervisor would miss; signals procedural fairness
  • Risks — contextually unsafe in low-trust cultures (peers protect or attack); time-consuming; dilutes accountability if used administratively (for pay) before raters are calibrated
  • Developmental vs administrative — start developmental-only. Only move it to pay/promotion after years of cultural readiness
  • Alex Sander — promoted high-performer; 360 surfaces collaboration deficits she dismisses; the lesson is that even accurate 360 data fails when the ratee and culture are not ready to act on it
  • Move — defend 360 only where (a) raters trust each other, (b) it is initially developmental, (c) the org can absorb the feedback. Otherwise it backfires
Three Indicators — Traits · Behaviors · Outcomes S11 · core typology Framework
Three ways to describe "performance". Each has a different validity profile and a different failure mode. Most defensible systems blend them.
  • Traits — enduring personal characteristics; must clearly fit the job; risky because hard to defend legally and easy to bias by similarity
  • Behaviors — what the person actually does; observable on the job; the foundation for BARS, BOS and most modern systems
  • Outcomes — results delivered; MBO/SMART territory; clean numbers but they can miss the "how" (an outcome-only system rewards cheating)
  • Move — defend the right blend for the job. Outcomes alone misses how; behaviors alone misses results; traits alone is the weakest single base
BARS — Behaviorally Anchored Rating Scales Smith & Kendall · S11 Framework
Each numeric level on the scale is anchored to a concrete critical-incident behavior. The result is a subjective method with much less rater drift.
  • Build process — (1) collect critical incidents from job experts, (2) sort them into performance dimensions, (3) scale each incident, (4) use them as numeric anchors
  • Strength — concrete, behavior-based, low rater drift, defensible in legal settings, useful for feedback
  • Cost — expensive to develop per job; needs re-validation as roles evolve
  • Move — cite as the gold-standard subjective method. When the case argues "ratings are noisy", BARS is the immediate prescription
BOS — Behavioral Observation Scales Latham · Ch10 Framework
A variant on BARS that asks how often each effective behavior occurs. Simpler to use day-to-day; scored as a sum of frequencies.
  • How — list of effective behaviors; rater scores each on a 1–5 frequency scale ("almost never" → "almost always"); behavior scores are summed
  • Strength — easier for raters than BARS; emphasises frequency rather than a single critical incident; supports developmental feedback
  • Limit — still subjective; frequency ratings can be biased; needs frame-of-reference training
  • Move — pitch BOS as the practical cousin of BARS when the case worries that BARS is too expensive to maintain
Management by Objectives (MBO) Drucker · Ch10 · S13 Framework
Manager and employee jointly set specific objectives at the start of the period. Evaluation = attainment vs the objectives. The outcomes-based backbone of modern PM.
  • Process — joint goal-setting → ongoing review → year-end evaluation against the agreed targets
  • Strength — alignment with strategy, employee buy-in (they helped set the goals), clean numeric base for pay
  • Limit — invites "what gets measured gets gamed"; can ignore behaviors and citizenship; can punish people for goals overtaken by events
  • Move — outcomes-based PM; always pair with SMART goal-setting and with a behaviors-based component to balance the "how"
SMART Goals Doran · S13 Framework
The operational tool that makes MBO workable. Five criteria a goal has to meet to be useful for managing — and to be defensible in a low-performer conversation.
  • Specific — narrow, concrete; not "improve service" but "reduce escalations from segment X"
  • Measurable — countable; lets you tell hit from miss without argument
  • Attainable — stretch but not fantasy; otherwise demotivates
  • Relevant — links to strategy and the role; not busywork
  • Time-bound — there is a date; otherwise it slides
  • Move — the operational tool when managing low performers. Pair with MBO; cite both in any case where the goals are vague or unmeasurable
Absolute vs Relative Judgment S11 · S12 Framework
Two appraisal logics. Pick by the differentiation problem you are trying to solve.
  • Absolute — every employee rated against a fixed standard; no comparison; clean for developmental use; vulnerable to inflation
  • Relative — rank-order, paired comparison, forced distribution; forces differentiation; vulnerable to team-level unfairness
  • Move — Vitality moved absolute → relative to break rating inflation. The decision is about which failure mode you can tolerate, not which is "correct"
Blake & Mouton's Managerial Grid Blake & Mouton (1964) · S13 Framework
A 9×9 grid of management style: concern for people on one axis, concern for task on the other. The five named corners frame the coaching-vs-pushing trade-off in low-performer conversations.
  • (1,1) Impoverished — minimal effort on either dimension; the disengaged manager
  • (1,9) Country Club — high people, low task; everyone likes you, nothing ships
  • (9,1) Authority-Compliance — high task, low people; you ship, but you burn the team
  • (5,5) Middle-of-the-Road — adequate on both; "balanced mediocrity"
  • (9,9) Team Management — high on both; demanding goals delivered through committed people. The recommended pole for coaching low performers
  • Move — frame any coaching-vs-pushing trade-off as a move on the grid. The bad manager is usually drifting toward (1,9) or (9,1); the move is back toward (9,9)
SBI-Next Feedback Framework CCL · S14 Framework
Structured, behavior-focused feedback in four steps. Designed to remove inference and judgement from a hard conversation, and to land with an agreed next step.
  • Situation — "In yesterday's client call..." — concrete time and place; not a generalisation
  • Behavior — "...you interrupted the client three times before they finished" — what you observed; no inference about intent
  • Impact — "...the client visibly stopped engaging and the meeting ran short" — effect on you / team / outcome
  • Next — "Next call let's agree you take notes for the first 10 minutes and only speak when asked" — agreed next step
  • Move — concrete tool for the McDonald's / peer-feedback role-plays. When the case asks "how would you have the conversation?", structure your answer with SBI-Next
Feedback Meeting — Before · During · After S14 · the conversation arc Practice
A feedback meeting is not just the meeting — it is what you prepared, what you said, and what you committed to follow up on. Skip any of the three and the conversation underperforms.
  • Before — collect specific examples; choose a private setting; check your own state; warn the employee on topic, not on verdict
  • During — open with intent; deliver the SBI-Next; listen actively; separate behavior from person; agree concrete next steps and dates
  • After — document; follow up on commitments; treat the next-step check-in as part of the cycle, not a "nice to have"
  • Move — when the case is a feedback role-play, structure your answer as Before / During / After. It signals you treat the meeting as a process, not a moment
Rating-Error Typology Ch10 · core taxonomy Framework
A canonical list of systematic cognitive biases that distort subjective ratings. Naming them is half the move; the other half is prescribing frame-of-reference training as the fix.
  • Halo — one strong trait (likeability, recent win) colors all ratings up or down
  • Leniency / Severity — rater systematically too soft or too hard; turns the scale into a constant
  • Central tendency — clusters everyone in the middle; avoids difficult conversations
  • Contrast — current ratee is judged relative to the one just rated, not the standard
  • Recency — over-weights the last few months at the expense of the full period
  • Similar-to-me — favors people who look, sound, or think like the rater
  • Move — name the bias, then prescribe frame-of-reference training as the standard fix. Cite as the reason subjective ratings without rater training drift unreliable
Frame-of-Reference Training Ch10 · the canonical rater-error fix Framework
Calibration training that teaches raters a shared definition of each performance level. The standard remedy for halo, leniency, central tendency and the rest.
  • How it works — (1) define performance dimensions, (2) show raters sample behaviors at each scale point, (3) have raters score practice cases, (4) discuss disagreements and recalibrate
  • Effect — narrows rater disagreement, raises validity of ratings, reduces all of the major rating errors in the typology
  • Limit — wears off without refresher training; can't fix structural problems (e.g. forced-distribution unfairness)
  • Move — every "rater bias" case in this block has FoR training as part of the answer. It is the calibration intervention, not a soft "training day"
Relevance · Deficiency · Contamination Ch10 · S11 · measurement-quality lens Framework
A Venn-style audit for any performance metric. The metric should overlap with "true performance" — and the two error modes are missing pieces of it (deficiency) and capturing things that aren't it (contamination).
  • Relevance — the overlap; the metric captures actual performance
  • Deficiency — the metric misses important parts of performance (false negative; e.g. a sales-only metric for a role that also requires mentoring)
  • Contamination — the metric captures irrelevant variance (false positive; e.g. customer satisfaction scores that depend on territory, not on the rep)
  • Add-ons — the metric also has to be reliable (consistent), valid (measures what it claims), and practical (affordable to collect)
  • Move — audit any KPI in a case with the Venn diagram. "Is this metric deficient? Is it contaminated?" usually surfaces the redesign in one move
Mixed Standard Scale Blanz & Ghiselli (1972) · Ch10 Framework
A subjective rating format that mixes good/average/poor behavioral statements in a single list, scored against a key. Designed to reduce halo and leniency.
  • How — the rater reads a shuffled list of "good", "average" and "poor" behavioral statements and indicates whether each describes the ratee. A scoring key converts responses into a rating
  • Strength — harder for the rater to game; reduces halo because each item stands alone; quick to administer
  • Limit — opaque to the ratee; harder to use for feedback because the scoring logic is hidden
  • Move — name as an alternative subjective format when standard rating scales drift. Less common than BARS/BOS but worth knowing as a Block-4 obscure citation
Comparative Procedures Ch10 · the family of relative methods Framework
The relative-judgement methods grouped as a family. Pick by team size and rater patience.
  • Simple ranking — order all employees top to bottom; clean but unwieldy past ~15
  • Alternation ranking — pick best, then worst, then second-best, second-worst, until you converge on the middle
  • Paired comparison — compare every possible pair; very accurate but n×(n-1)/2 comparisons gets expensive fast
  • Forced distribution — allocate fixed percentages to categories (the Vitality move); fastest at scale
  • Move — choose by team size and rater patience. In a 6-person team, paired comparison is feasible; at 600, forced distribution is the only one that scales
Deloitte's Reinvented PM HBR (Buckingham & Goodall) · S11 video Case
Deloitte killed annual ratings, replaced them with weekly check-ins, and reduced the year-end conversation to four future-focused statements per employee from the team leader.
  • The shift — kill the annual rating, replace with weekly check-ins between team leader and team member, plus four future-focused statements per employee, per period
  • Why future-focused — past ratings are noisy and demotivating; what the team leader will do next is the actionable signal
  • Why the team leader — closest observer; reduces the HR-form-filling layer; ties the data to decisions only the team leader can make
  • Move — cite as the practitioner example of moving from appraisal → management. The Deloitte case is the modern bookend to Aguinis' theory

  • McDonald's / peer feedback role-plays — the venue for SBI-Next and the Before/During/After framing. The exam reward is structure: name SBI explicitly, lay out the three phases of the conversation, and close with a documented next step.

Rater errors to name (and how to fix)

  • Halo — one trait colors all ratings · Leniency / Severity — too soft / too hard
  • Central tendency — clusters everyone in the middle · Contrast — judging vs others just rated
  • Recency — over-weights last few months · Similar-to-me — favors people like the rater
  • Fix — frame-of-reference training calibrates raters and curbs systematic errors
  • Structural backup — pair rater training with a behavior-anchored format (BARS / BOS) and with relative procedures where inflation is the dominant failure mode

Exam moves

  • Cite Aguinis to reframe "we hate PM" as "we do appraisal poorly". The fix is to slide the system toward the management end of the dial — continuous, line-manager-owned, future-focused.
  • For any rating-design case, name what is being measured (trait / behavior / outcome) AND how (absolute vs relative). Two dimensions in one move signals you can see the design space.
  • Use Vitality Health as your go-to case for moving from absolute to relative ranking — but flag the team-cohesion cost and the manager-training requirement as the open tensions.
  • Argue 360 feedback only where culture supports honest peer feedback (Alex Sander = it didn't), and only in a developmental capacity until raters are calibrated.
  • Cite Deloitte's four future-focused statements as the practitioner example of killing ratings. It pairs cleanly with Aguinis and shows you read beyond the academic literature.
  • When the case is a feedback conversation, structure your answer as SBI-Next inside the Before / During / After arc. Examiners reward structure plus a documented next step.
  • Audit any KPI in a case with Relevance · Deficiency · Contamination. Naming the deficient or contaminated part usually surfaces the redesign in one sentence.
  • Name the rater error explicitly (halo / leniency / central tendency / contrast / recency / similar-to-me) before prescribing frame-of-reference training. The diagnosis-then-treatment sequence is the move.
  • For low-performer coaching, plot the manager on Blake & Mouton's grid. The fix is usually a move from (1,9) or (9,1) toward (9,9): demand the goal, support the person.
  • Pair MBO with SMART every time goals come up. Outcomes-based PM without SMART criteria is just a wish list.

05Pay, incentives & employment relations

Pay is the loudest signal an organisation sends. Who you attract, who you keep, what they do once inside — all of it is downstream of the design choices you make about money, benefits and the relational extras that surround them.

Block 5 is built on a single foundational diagnostic — Milkovich, Newman & Gerhart's Pay Model — and a handful of frameworks that hang off it. The Pay Model gives you a three-layer stack (Objectives → Policies → Techniques) that lets you take apart any compensation system on the planet. The Total Returns framework reminds you that cash is only part of the package. The Four Criteria turn the abstract policy choices into an operational scorecard. And Gerhart & Rynes' Incentive vs Sorting Effect distinction is the under-cited insight that pay also sorts who walks in the door — not just how hard people work once they are inside.

Surrounding all this are the design choices: merit vs incentive, the three levels of incentive plans, the three shapes of pay structure, and the institutional comparator (Hall & Soskice's Varieties of Capitalism) that explains why what works in Stuttgart will not necessarily work in Houston. Plus a critical-appraisal toolkit (Caveat Emptor) so you don't get fooled by the next "compensation X drives outcome Y" claim that crosses your desk.

The exam questions in this block tend to be deceptively open: "What are the four great basic areas to evaluate a compensation policy?" · "Difference between merit and incentive?" · "Explain sorting vs incentive effect." Short prompts, big surface area. The way to win is to anchor in the Pay Model, layer in the Four Criteria, then bring in whichever specific framework the prompt is pointing at. The rest of this chapter walks each framework end-to-end so you can do exactly that.

The frameworks worth knowing cold

The Pay Model — three layers Milkovich, Newman & Gerhart · Ch1 The Pay Model Core
Objectives drive policies, which drive techniques. Any pay system on earth can be diagnosed against this three-layer stack — the most important single framework in Block 5.
  • Layer 1 — Objectives (the "why") — every pay system serves four objectives. Efficiency (does pay support performance, quality, customer focus and cost control?), Fairness (is the system seen as just by both procedure and outcome?), Ethics (does it respect employees' dignity, e.g. living wage, transparent rules?), and Compliance (does it meet the law — minimum wage, anti-discrimination, working time, tax)
  • Layer 2 — Policy choices (the "what") — the four pay-policy levers any organisation must set. Internal alignment (how do pay relationships inside the firm compare across jobs and levels?), External competitiveness (how does our pay compare to the outside market?), Employee contributions (how do we recognise differences in individual performance, seniority or skill?), and Management (how do we plan, budget, communicate and evaluate the pay system itself?)
  • Layer 3 — Techniques (the "how") — the concrete HR tools that turn policy into action. Job analysis and evaluation feed internal alignment; salary surveys feed external competitiveness; pay structures (grade and range design) translate both into a usable map; performance-based pay wires the contributions policy to behaviour; communication closes the loop so employees actually understand the system they are in
  • The diagnostic move — when a pay system is failing, ask which layer is broken: are objectives unclear (no shared "why")? Are policies internally contradictory (e.g. external benchmarking but no internal equity check)? Or are techniques poorly executed (out-of-date surveys, no job analysis)?
  • Why it is the master framework — every other Block 5 framework slots inside it. The Four Criteria operationalise the policy layer; merit vs incentive is a technique choice; sorting vs incentive effect explains how pay achieves the efficiency objective; Hall & Soskice explains why policy choices vary by country
Total Returns / Total Compensation Milkovich, Newman & Gerhart · Ch1 Core
Reward = cash + benefits + relational returns. Total Compensation is the transactional half; Total Returns is the whole package. Don't conflate them.
  • Total Compensation (transactional half) — everything monetary that can be totalled on a payslip or benefit statement: base pay (the fixed rate for the job), merit pay and COLA (cost-of-living adjustments that lift base), short-term incentives (annual bonus, gain-sharing), long-term incentives (stock, options, multi-year cash plans), and benefits (health, pension, paid leave, perks)
  • Relational / psychological returns — everything that matters but doesn't show on the payslip: recognition and status, employment security, challenging work, learning and growth, and leadership / mentoring opportunities. These are the half of the reward picture that pure cash analyses miss
  • Why the distinction matters — Total Compensation is what you cost the firm and what an offer letter quotes. Total Returns is what determines whether the employee actually feels rewarded. A high-paying job with no growth or recognition can lose to a slightly lower-paying one with both
  • The exam move — when pay alone won't fix a retention or motivation problem, redesign the whole return mix. Conversely, when a firm cannot raise base pay (see Zero Wage Increase), the relational half is the only lever left
  • Confusion to avoid — Total Compensation ≠ Total Returns. The first is a subset of the second. Mix them up in the exam and you signal you haven't read Ch1 properly
Four Criteria for a Competitive Compensation System Milkovich, Newman & Gerhart · Ch1 · Session 15 Core
The operational scorecard that turns the Pay Model's abstract policy choices into a four-question audit. The single most usable framework for any "evaluate this compensation system" prompt.
  • 1 — Internal equity — equal pay for jobs of equal worth inside the organisation. Tested via job analysis (what does the role actually do?) and job evaluation (how do roles compare on skill, effort, responsibility, working conditions?). A firm where two equivalent roles are paid 20% apart for no reason fails this test. Maps to the Internal alignment policy choice in the Pay Model
  • 2 — External equity — pay is at par with the relevant labour market. Tested via salary surveys (market data, industry benchmarks) and a chosen position relative to market (lag, match or lead the market). A firm paying 25% below market on a hot skill loses talent before it can even hire. Maps to External competitiveness
  • 3 — Performance link — pay is tied to performance, so contribution differences translate into reward differences. Tested by asking: do top performers earn meaningfully more than average performers? Is the link visible to employees? Maps to Employee contributions
  • 4 — Cost / benefit — the system is affordable and its returns are measurable. Tested by total compensation cost as % of revenue, by ROI on incentive spend, and by whether the pay system can survive a downturn. Maps to Management
  • How to use it in an exam — score the system on each of the four. Pinpoint which is failing. Recommend the technique that fixes it (job analysis for internal equity, surveys for external, redesigned incentives for performance link, plan reform for cost/benefit). This is the operational answer to "what are the four great basic areas to evaluate a compensation policy?"
  • Watch the trade-offs — these four criteria can conflict. Raising external competitiveness on hot skills can break internal equity. Tightening cost/benefit can weaken the performance link. The art of pay design is finding the policy mix where the four sit in tolerable balance
Pay-for-Performance — Merit vs Incentive Session 15 · BAG Grocers · Zero Wage Increase cases Core
Two very different ways to tie pay to performance. Merit adds to base (sticky and cumulative); incentives are re-earned each period (variable and reversible). The choice between them is one of the most consequential calls a compensation designer makes.
  • Merit increase — the mechanics — a permanent addition to base pay, granted (typically annually) on the basis of past performance ratings. Once granted it compounds: this year's merit also raises the base on which next year's merit is calculated. It is also the base used for pensions, severance and most benefits
  • Merit — the strengths — signals long-term commitment, builds employee security, easy to administer alongside the appraisal cycle, retains good performers by raising their exit cost
  • Merit — the weaknesses — once you give it, you almost never take it back even if performance drops; the cumulative cost grows even when business doesn't; psychologically the lift quickly becomes "what I earn" rather than "a reward"; the link to specific behaviour is weak (you can't really tell what you did to get the 3% vs the 2%)
  • Incentive — the mechanics — a variable payment that must be re-earned each period. Targets and payout rules are specified in advance: hit X, get Y. Incentive does not roll into base. A bad year zeros it out
  • Incentive — the strengths — directly tied to specified behaviour; cost scales with results (cheaper in bad years, expensive only when the firm can afford it); reversible; sharp motivational signal
  • Incentive — the weaknesses — designing the right targets is hard; can drive gaming and short-termism; can crowd out cooperation if individually structured; less predictability for the employee
  • BAG Grocers — the case where senior leaders debate whether to swap (or supplement) sticky merit raises with re-earned incentives in a low-margin retail context. The answer turns on cost containment vs employee security
  • Zero Wage Increase — the case where the firm cannot give a raise of either kind. Forces you to think about the relational half of Total Returns: recognition, learning, work design, employment security — the non-cash levers when cash is off the table
Incentive Effect & Sorting Effect of Pay Gerhart & Rynes Key
Pay shapes performance through two mechanisms, not one. The first is obvious. The second is the killer cite — and the evidence says it's the bigger lever.
  • Incentive effect — the textbook story — well-designed pay motivates the people currently in the building to work harder, smarter or longer. This is the mechanism every "money motivates" debate is about. It assumes a fixed workforce and looks only at within-person behaviour change
  • Sorting effect — the under-cited story — pay structures also change who applies, who accepts, who stays and who leaves. High-powered, performance-linked pay attracts (and retains) high performers; flat, security-focused pay attracts those who value stability. Self-selection does most of the work, quietly
  • The glass-installation evidence — Gerhart & Rynes cite a study where a firm switched from hourly pay to piece-rate and productivity rose 44%. The breakdown is the kicker: the majority of the gain came not from the same workers trying harder, but from better workers self-selecting in and weaker ones self-selecting out. Pay had changed the workforce, not just the effort
  • Why this matters — every estimate of "pay effects" that doesn't separate incentive from sorting will understate the true value of pay design. Most studies look at within-person change and miss the bigger composition story
  • The exam move — when someone says "but money doesn't really motivate", reach for sorting. Even if pure incentive effects were zero (they aren't), pay would still matter because it changes who you have. This is the killer counter-move
  • Design implication — if you want to change your workforce composition (e.g. attract sales-driven hunters rather than relationship farmers), redesign pay, not just recruitment

Types of Incentive Plans Session 15 Key
Three levels of aggregation — individual, group, organisation. The right level depends on what behaviour you want to incentivise and how cleanly you can measure it.
  • Individual incentives — paid on personal output or behaviour. Examples: commissions (sales), piece-rate (manufacturing, glass installation), individual bonuses (hitting personal KPIs), awards (employee of the month). Strongest behavioural link; worst for cooperation. Use when the work is individually measurable and cooperation isn't critical
  • Group incentives — paid on team or unit performance. Examples: team bonuses, gain-sharing (the team shares productivity gains above a baseline). Reinforces cooperation and peer monitoring; weaker individual link (free-rider risk grows with team size). Use when output is genuinely interdependent
  • Organization-level incentives — paid on firm-wide results. Examples: profit-sharing, ESOPs (employee stock ownership plans), stock options, long-term incentive plans. Aligns everyone with firm performance and builds an ownership mindset; weakest individual line-of-sight (any one employee's effort barely moves the share price). Use for senior leadership and for cultural alignment
  • The design principle — match the level of aggregation to the level at which the behaviour you want actually plays out. Don't pay individually for work that requires teamwork. Don't expect a profit-sharing scheme to motivate a junior contributor whose effort is invisible in the financials
  • Mixing levels — most mature pay systems blend all three: individual incentives for line-of-sight motivation, group incentives for cooperation, organization incentives for alignment and retention. The mix should reflect the strategy
Kinds of Pay Structure Session 16 Key
Three structural shapes for pay grades. The shape of your structure shapes the careers people can have inside it.
  • Narrow-graded structure — many grades, each with a relatively short pay range. Movement requires promotion from one grade to the next, often through formal job evaluation. Fits tall hierarchies, public sector, traditional banks. Strengths: clear career steps, easy to administer. Weaknesses: encourages "title inflation" since the only way to earn more is to be re-graded; rigid
  • Broad-graded structure (broadbanding) — far fewer grades, each spanning a wide pay range. Multiple roles and career steps sit inside one band. Movement happens through within-band progression, not promotion. Fits flatter organisations, professional firms, modern tech. Strengths: flexibility, supports lateral careers, removes obsession with grade. Weaknesses: less clear "next step", needs strong managers to use the range well
  • Job family structure — pay grouped by occupational family (e.g. engineering, sales, finance, operations), each family with its own range and progression rules. Fits firms with very different labour markets across families (software engineers vs operations). Strengths: lets external competitiveness vary by family; respects market reality. Weaknesses: can break internal equity across families ("why does engineering get paid more than ops?")
  • The design link — narrow-graded structures emphasise internal equity; broad-graded structures emphasise flexibility and contribution; job-family structures emphasise external competitiveness. Each maps to a different Pay Model policy emphasis
  • The exam move — when a salary-negotiation case asks "is this offer fair?", check which structure type you are inside. The answer is shaped by what the structure even lets the firm offer
Varieties of Capitalism — CMEs vs LMEs Hall & Soskice (2001) Key
The comparative-institutions framework that explains why "best practice" in pay is country-specific. There is no universal pay design — only designs that fit the institutional environment they sit in.
  • Coordinated Market Economies (CMEs) — firms coordinate via non-market mechanisms: industry-level collective bargaining, employer associations, dual vocational training (apprenticeships), strong works councils, codetermination. Wages compressed within sectors; mobility lower; investment in firm-specific skills high. Germany is the canonical example, with the Nordics and Japan close cousins
  • Liberal Market Economies (LMEs) — firms coordinate via competitive markets and hierarchy: firm-level bargaining (where any), weak unions, general-skills education, fluid labour markets, fast hire/fire. Wages dispersed; high-powered incentives common; investment in general transferable skills. The US is canonical, with the UK, Ireland and Canada in the same family
  • Implication for pay design — a high-variance, individual-incentive-heavy plan that works in California will run into legal, institutional and cultural friction in Germany (where sector agreements set the floor, works councils have a say, and pay compression is the norm). Conversely, a Germany-style narrow band with collective progression rules will leak top talent in an LME
  • The institutional complementarity argument — Hall & Soskice's deeper point: institutions reinforce each other. CME pay practices complement CME training systems and CME corporate governance. You can't import one piece in isolation
  • The exam move — whenever the question asks "should this firm use plan X?", ask which institutional system the firm operates in. Cite VoC to argue the answer is country-specific. Especially powerful when a US-based case discusses German or Nordic operations (or vice versa)

Direct vs Indirect Compensation Session 15 Supporting
A finer-grained typology of the transactional half of Total Returns. Useful when "just raise base pay" needs to be challenged with the full mix.
  • Direct compensation — cash that hits the bank account directly. Includes basic pay (the fixed rate for the job), variable pay (bonuses, commissions, incentives), and profit-sharing (share of firm-wide profits paid as cash)
  • Indirect monetary compensation — has cash value but the employee doesn't see it on the payslip. Includes health insurance, pension / retirement plans, life insurance, disability cover, and statutory contributions
  • Indirect non-monetary compensation — has real value but no easy cash equivalent. Includes flexible working, telecommuting, flexi-hours, childcare facilities, paid leave beyond statutory minimum, and well-being programmes
  • Why the typology matters — when a firm is told "you just need to pay more", this framework breaks "pay" into three buckets and shows where the real lever might be. Increasing indirect non-monetary compensation (flex, well-being) is often cheaper per unit of perceived value than raising base
  • The exam move — defend the full compensation mix against the "just raise base pay" simplification. Pair with Total Returns for the relational layer
Likelihood-to-Unionize Matrix Session 16 Supporting
A 2×2 that predicts which worker groups are most likely to unionise. Useful for any "who will unionise next" question, and for thinking about how pay/benefits design interacts with employee voice.
  • The axes — benefit of unionising (how much would unionisation actually improve pay, conditions or security for this group?) × ability to organise (how feasible is collective action — concentrated workplace, shared schedule, low employer hostility?)
  • High benefit + high ability — blue-collar manufacturing, public-sector employees, transport workers. These are the historically heavily unionised groups
  • Low benefit + high ability — many white-collar professional workers. Can organise but get enough through individual bargaining or firm-set rewards that unionising offers limited marginal benefit
  • High benefit + low ability — gig workers, temporary workers, app-based platform workers. The group that would benefit most from collective bargaining but faces structural barriers (no shared workplace, no permanent contract, often classified as independent contractors). The growing edge of labour organising in advanced economies
  • Low benefit + low ability — the self-employed and small business owners. Neither willing nor structurally able to unionise
  • The exam move — predict where unionisation will rise next: the high-benefit / low-ability quadrant is where institutional creativity is needed (sectoral bargaining, worker centres, gig-platform legislation)
Caveat Emptor — Informed Consumer of Research Milkovich, Newman & Gerhart · Ch1 (Circuit City) Supporting
A three-question critical-appraisal model for any claim of the form "compensation practice X drives outcome Y". The intellectual hygiene needed to avoid copying the wrong best practice.
  • Question 1 — Useful? Does the finding actually apply to my context? A study on glass installers in the US Midwest may not generalise to knowledge workers in Madrid. Industry, country, role, period, sample size — all matter
  • Question 2 — Causation? Is the relationship truly causal, or just correlational? Cross-sectional studies routinely confuse "high-pay firms perform well" with "pay drives performance". Maybe well-performing firms can afford to pay more (reverse causality). Maybe both are driven by a third factor (e.g. management quality)
  • Question 3 — Alternatives? What other explanations fit the same data? If a firm cut wages and profits rose, was it the wage cut — or was it the simultaneous restructuring, the new product, the macro tailwind?
  • Circuit City — the worked example — Ch1 uses Circuit City's wage-cutting story to show how easily a confident causal claim ("we cut wages, profits rose, therefore wage cuts cause profit growth") falls apart under the three questions. Useful? (Was this even a representative case?) Causation? (Or coincidence?) Alternatives? (Or did the firm decline because it lost its best people?)
  • The exam move — apply to any "compensation X drives Y" claim a case throws at you. The three questions are a one-paragraph critical lens that signals graduate-level reasoning
WorldatWork Total Rewards Model Milkovich Ch1 (footnote 28) · WorldatWork Supporting
The industry-standard practitioner version of Total Returns — five reward buckets that HR professionals actually use to design and communicate the whole package.
  • 1 — Compensation — base pay, variable pay, short- and long-term incentives. The transactional cash core
  • 2 — Benefits — health, retirement, paid time off, statutory and elective benefits. The indirect monetary layer
  • 3 — Work-life — flexible scheduling, remote work, well-being, family support, paid leave. The indirect non-monetary layer
  • 4 — Performance & Recognition — formal recognition programmes, performance management, awards. The "we see and value what you do" layer
  • 5 — Development & Career — training, mentoring, tuition support, promotion paths, learning opportunities. The investment-in-you layer
  • Relationship to Total Returns — the WorldatWork five buckets map cleanly onto Milkovich's transactional/relational split. Buckets 1 and 2 are transactional; 3, 4, 5 are relational. Use WorldatWork as the practitioner-friendly version when you want a more granular communication-ready taxonomy
  • The exam move — cite WorldatWork as the practitioner parallel to Total Returns when a question is about "designing a rewards strategy" rather than diagnosing a system

The cases — what each one tests

  • BAG Grocers (Merit Pay Decisions at BAG) — the merit-vs-incentive case. Low-margin grocery chain debates whether to redesign its sticky annual merit raises in favour of variable incentives. Tests the trade-off between employee security (merit) and cost discipline + performance link (incentive). The answer turns on what behaviour the firm actually needs (loyalty? differentiated performance?) and what its cost structure can sustain
  • Zero Wage Increase (Harvard 20W11154) — the case where neither merit nor incentive is on the table because the firm cannot raise wages at all. Forces you to redesign the relational half of Total Returns: recognition, work design, learning, employment security, transparent communication. The answer to "what do you do when you can't pay more?"
  • Circuit City (Caveat Emptor exercise) — the case Ch1 uses to drill the three critical-appraisal questions. Circuit City's confident "we cut wages and profits rose" story collapses when you ask Useful? Causation? Alternatives? The training case for not being a naive consumer of compensation research

The trade-offs the Pay Model forces you to face

  • Internal equity ↔ External competitiveness — paying market for a hot skill can blow up internal pay relationships. Resolve by deciding which policy dominates by job family
  • Merit (stability) ↔ Incentive (variability) — security signals build commitment; variability builds performance link. Most mature systems blend both
  • Individual ↔ Group incentives — individual sharpens motivation; group reinforces cooperation. Match to interdependence of the work
  • Transactional ↔ Relational rewards — cash is universal but expensive; relational returns are cheap per unit of perceived value but harder to design and signal
  • CME ↔ LME logic — high-powered, dispersed pay fits liberal market economies; compressed, collectively-set pay fits coordinated market economies. The wrong logic breaks both ways

Exam moves

  • Open every compensation question by naming the Pay Model's three layers (objectives → policy choices → techniques). It gives you instant structure and signals you know the foundational framework. Then drop into whichever layer the question is actually about.
  • For "four basic areas to evaluate a compensation policy" — answer with the Four Criteria: internal equity, external equity, performance link, cost/benefit. Each is a question you can score the system on. Bonus marks for mapping each criterion to its Pay Model policy choice.
  • Merit vs incentive in one line — merit is a sticky base lift rewarding past behaviour; incentive is a re-earned variable payment for future behaviour. Merit raises cost permanently; incentives scale cost with results. Use BAG Grocers as your worked example.
  • Sorting vs incentive effect — the killer cite — incentive effect = pay motivates the people you have (how hard they work); sorting effect = pay changes who you have (who walks in the door). Cite Gerhart & Rynes' 44% glass-installation finding; the majority came from sorting, not incentive. Devastating counter-move to "money doesn't motivate".
  • Total Compensation ≠ Total Returns — Total Compensation is the transactional half (cash + benefits); Total Returns adds the relational half (recognition, security, challenging work, learning). When pay alone can't fix the problem (or pay isn't available — see Zero Wage Increase), redesign the relational half.
  • Use Hall & Soskice (VoC) whenever the question involves comparing countries or considering whether a US-style plan will work in Europe. CMEs (Germany, Nordics) compress pay through sectoral bargaining; LMEs (US, UK) disperse pay through firm-level high-powered incentives. The right design is country-specific.
  • Match the incentive level to the behaviour — individual incentives for individually-measurable work, group incentives for genuinely interdependent work, organization incentives for cultural alignment and leadership. Don't pay individually for what requires teamwork.
  • For pay structure questions — narrow-graded = many grades, hierarchical, public-sector flavour; broad-graded = flatter, broadbanded, professional firms; job-family = lets external market vary by occupation. Each emphasises a different Pay Model policy.
  • Apply Caveat Emptor to any "compensation practice X causes outcome Y" claim. Three questions: Useful in my context? True causation, or just correlation? Alternative explanations? Circuit City is the worked example.
  • Cite WorldatWork's five buckets (compensation, benefits, work-life, performance/recognition, development/career) as the practitioner parallel to Total Returns when the question is "design a rewards strategy" rather than "diagnose a system".
Chapter six · Block 6

06Unions, collective bargaining & the high road

Pfeffer's quiet provocation: good HR doesn't replace unions. Sometimes it needs them.

Block 6 inverts the conventional wisdom. The standard MBA-school claim — "if you're unionized, your HR failed" — becomes the thesis Pfeffer sets up to dismantle. His counter-claim is sharper than it first looks: unions raise wages, yes, but the feedback effects (skill, lower turnover, capital deepening, productivity) usually offset the cost. And union contracts lock in high-commitment practices that would otherwise erode the first time a recession arrived and the CFO asked for cuts. That second move — unions as a countervailing force against management backsliding — is the one examiners reward.

The block also gives you a vivid counter-example. Tepperman's (Ivey case 9B03C028) is the picture of what happens when bargaining breaks down: a committee-level deal rejected 23-4, a walkout at 8:30 a.m. the next morning, nails on the parking lot, and a 75-year "Somebody Cares" identity ripped apart. The contrast with Pfeffer's positive cases — Ford under Petersen, Saturn, Kaiser-Permanente, Southwest, UPS, Cingular — is the whole essay structure: cooperative bargaining produces joint gains, adversarial bargaining shrinks them.

This is also the block where the macro frame matters most. If an essay asks why advanced economies can't win on labor cost, you have High Road vs Low Road waiting: stop fighting Bangladesh on price; compete on innovation, productivity, brainpower — and use HPWS to do it. Unions support that path because the contract protects the training budget when the next quarter looks ugly.

The frameworks worth knowing cold

Union Wage Effect & Indirect/Feedback Effects Pfeffer (drawing on Freeman & Medoff) Core
Unions raise wages — but higher wages don't automatically lower profits or competitiveness. Four feedback loops convert wages into productivity.
  • The four feedback moves (memorise the sequence):
    1. Skill in — "you get what you pay for". Higher wages attract more skilled, more productive applicants.
    2. Turnover out — multiple studies (union and non-union) show a negative relationship between relative wages and quit rates. Lower turnover saves direct replacement cost plus the loss of experience and tacit knowledge.
    3. Capital deepening — firms facing higher labor costs substitute capital, technology, and efficient work methods for labor.
    4. Training intensifies — firms invest more in the workers they're now paying more for. Construction-industry studies show unionized workforces are more productive than nonunion counterparts.
  • Net result — productivity gains from a more skilled, experienced, well-equipped workforce can completely outweigh the extra wage costs. Total cost is roughly unaffected; productivity is higher.
  • Boundary condition (don't forget this) — union effects on profitability bite mainly where firms hold monopoly or substantial market power. In competitive markets there is essentially little or no union effect on profitability; under monopoly, unionization simply shares some of the monopoly rent with workers rather than only owners.
  • Where the framework comes from — Pfeffer is drawing on Freeman & Medoff (the foundational labor-economics work). The "indirect effects" label is theirs.
  • Exam move — when you see "do unions hurt company performance?", lead with this loop. The wage-vs-profit trade-off is overstated because wages → productivity through these four channels.
Unions & High-Commitment / High-Performance Work Systems Pfeffer Core
Unionization is positively associated with high-commitment practices — and contracts make those practices durable. Unions don't kill HPWS; they entrench it.
  • The reframe — the lazy story says HPWS and unions are substitutes ("good HR makes unions unnecessary"). Pfeffer says they're complements. High-commitment arrangements are fragile; the contract is what makes them survive a recession.
  • The fragility problem — high-commitment systems require sustained investment in training, time spent on teams, real (not nominal) job security, and information sharing. Under economic stress firms cut training first, then resort to layoffs (which are themselves often counterproductive). That's the "backsliding" Pfeffer warns about.
  • Why unions fix it — aspects of high-performance practices can be enshrined in the contractual language jointly negotiated. Once training budgets, team structures, no-layoff clauses, or grievance procedures are written into the contract, management can't walk them back unilaterally when the quarter looks bad.
  • Petersen / Ford / UAW — During the 1980s, CEO Donald Petersen implemented total quality management and transformed Ford's culture. He credited his ability to persevere through setbacks to the presence of the UAW — aspects of the change were embedded in the formal contract.
  • Saturn (GM) — At one point a model of union-management cooperation and a high-productivity producer of high-quality cars. People at Saturn credited the UAW contract with helping maintain the cooperative, high-performance work practices.
  • Kaiser-Permanente — Joint decision-making model launched 1997 across 26 local unions. Workplace injuries fell >20%, employee satisfaction climbed 65% → 80%, cost savings >$100M.
  • Southwest · UPS — Some of the most competitive private-sector firms are unionized. Southwest is the most unionized of all U.S. airlines and has been profitable each of the past 35 years. UPS is unionized too. Pfeffer's blunt point: "it is possible to be economically successful and have labor organizations at the same time."
  • Cingular Wireless & CWA — Cingular stayed neutral while 18,000 employees joined the Communication Workers of America, and simultaneously posted the best financial results in its history. The CWA even launched a campaign to market Cingular's service.
  • Exam move — flip the conventional wisdom: "Unions don't kill HPWS — they're the institutional infrastructure that makes HPWS survive cost pressure."
High Road vs Low Road Competitive Strategy Pfeffer Key
Two competitive paths. Only one is sustainable for advanced economies — and union backing is what makes it durable.
  • The low road — compete on labor cost. Squeeze wages, defer training, treat people as a variable input. The fatal flaw: there is always a cheaper country. Advanced economies can never win this race against Bangladesh, Vietnam, or wherever production migrates next.
  • The high road — stop competing on cost. Compete on innovation, productivity, quality, and brainpower. The HR machinery for the high road is HPWS (selective hiring, training, teams, security, information sharing) — the same bundle Pfeffer defends throughout his work.
  • Why unions matter here — the high road requires sustained investment in people. The temptation under quarterly earnings pressure is to abandon it. Unions are a countervailing force: contracts protect the training budget, lock in security, and resist short-term cost-cutting that would undermine the strategy.
  • Pfeffer's claim — "unionization is positively associated with high-commitment practices and may make a shift to the high road more likely and easier."
  • Exam move — frame any "should we be unionized?" question as a strategic choice. If you've already chosen the high road, unions are an ally; if you're on the low road, they're a constraint — but you're on the wrong road anyway.
"Management Gets the Union It Deserves" Thesis Pfeffer (the wisdom he sets up to dismantle) Key
The conventional wisdom: progressive HR forestalls unionization, so if you're unionized you failed at HR. Pfeffer's challenge: even good HR doesn't make unions redundant.
  • The claim — many leaders believe progressive HR practices both forestall unionization and make union representation unnecessary. If your workforce voted to organize, it's because you mismanaged. Pfeffer summarises: "management gets the union it deserves."
  • Why the wisdom feels plausible — people concede unions mattered historically for workplace safety, outlawing child labor, limiting working hours — but assume those reforms are now institutionalized in law, making contemporary union advocacy "less valuable."
  • Pfeffer's challenge — three counter-moves:
    1. Even excellent HR doesn't deliver the complementary protections a union provides — voice on the shop floor, grievance procedures, contractual guarantees.
    2. Many of those historical reforms persist because unions keep advocating; without them they would erode (see California nurse-staffing fights).
    3. Some of the most competitive firms (Southwest, UPS, Saturn, Kaiser) are heavily unionized, falsifying the "good HR forestalls" frame.
  • The Tepperman's nuance — Bill Tepperman was "neither pro- nor anti-union" and recognised the usefulness of a union in large corporations. The early-1980s unionization at Tepperman's did follow a period when Bill admitted he wasn't as involved with warehouse employees as he should have been — three drivers: feeling ignored, communication gaps, and a warehouse-director liaison who presented one persona to Bill and another to staff. In that sense the conventional wisdom held; in 2001 it didn't.
  • Exam move — use the thesis as the structural opener for any pro-union essay. "Conventional wisdom holds that X. Pfeffer's reading complicates this on three counts…"
Collective Bargaining as Power-Sharing / Joint Decision-Making Pfeffer Key
Bargaining isn't only a price negotiation. Done well, it is institutionalised communication and joint decision-making with front-line staff — and the cooperative version dramatically outperforms the adversarial one.
  • Two modes of bargaining — adversarial (zero-sum, distributive, each side maximises own share) vs cooperative (joint problem-solving, integrative, expand the pie before dividing it).
  • What cooperative bargaining actually does — it gives front-line staff a structured voice in operational decisions, not just wages. Joint committees on safety, staffing, scheduling, training. The information flow runs both ways.
  • Kaiser-Permanente partnership (1997-) — A $28B U.S. health maintenance organization. After years of adversarial relations including strikes, it launched a formal labor-management partnership with its 26 local unions using a joint decision-making model. Results where it took hold:
    • Workplace injuries fell by >20%
    • Employee satisfaction: 65% (2000) → 80% (2003)
    • Plan-member satisfaction up 3% (strong, because only the sick interact much between surveys)
    • Cost savings attributed to the partnership: >$100M
  • Healthcare evidence — the 5.5% mortality study — A 2004 study of risk-adjusted heart-attack death rates in 344 acute-care California hospitals found facilities with unionized nurses had a 5.5% lower mortality rate than non-union hospitals (after statistical controls). Mechanism: increased nurse power and joint decision-making over staffing.
  • Nurse-patient ratios — Studies summarised by the U.S. Institute of Medicine show lowering the nurse-patient ratio raises rates of patient infection, pneumonia, cardiac arrest, and death. California nurses lobbied for a staffing law; after passage they fought delays. Pfeffer notes pejorative "special interests" framing — but it's unclear why nurses are more "special interests" than the for-profit hospitals opposing the law.
  • The adversarial trap — much of the partnership gain disappears when relations are adversarial. Tepperman's is the cautionary case: a tentative deal at the committee level, then ratification collapsed, picket lines, nails on the parking lot. The pie didn't grow; both sides lost.
  • Exam move — defend partnership-style bargaining over adversarial. Adversarial shrinks joint gains; cooperative bargaining expands the pie and produces better operational outcomes (safety, quality, retention).

Union Effect on Wage Distribution / Egalitarian Compression Pfeffer Supporting
Unions compress the wage structure → less inequality and less discrimination. The bonus argument: wide pay dispersion tends to harm performance.
  • The egalitarian ethos — unions need internal solidarity to act collectively. That ethos drives them toward more equal wage structures (compressing the top-bottom gap inside the bargaining unit).
  • Discrimination effects — unionization is associated with smaller white–minority and men–women wage gaps. Posted scales and collectively negotiated rates leave less room for case-by-case discretion (and the bias that comes with it).
  • The macro picture — U.S. (and other countries') rising wage inequality — gap between highest and lowest deciles widening — has many causes, but one contributing factor has been the decline in unionization. Pfeffer's framing: to the extent inequality is a problem (political instability, alienation), unionization "provides a part of the solution."
  • The performance kicker (counter-intuitive) — evidence from baseball teams, university faculty, and top management teams shows that more variation in wages within a unit is negatively associated with performance. Wide dispersion undermines cohesion, fuels comparison stress, and depresses team output. This directly undercuts the strong "pay-for-performance with big spreads" argument.
  • Connection to Block 5 — pair this with Gerhart & Rynes (sorting vs incentive). Pay spreads can sort, yes — but very wide spreads also damage the unit through dispersion effects. There's a sweet spot, not "more is better."
  • Exam move — when an essay drifts to inequality or DEI, you have this argument waiting. Unions are part of the solution, not part of the problem.
Collective Bargaining Process — Negotiation, Tentative Deal, Ratification Tepperman's case (Ivey 9B03C028) Supporting
The structured negotiation arc, from positions to ratification — and the failure path when the membership rejects the committee's deal.
  • The negotiating committee (5 people in the Tepperman's case):
    • Union side — two elected union stewards (from the warehouse floor) + one business agent from the union local (here, the Canadian National Brotherhood of Teamsters).
    • Firm side — the warehouse director + legal counsel.
  • The process arc:
    1. Opening positions — each side states initial demands (pay, vacation, benefits, working conditions, sometimes firing a specific manager).
    2. Bargaining — "in the spirit of negotiations, both sides move from their initial positions." This is the integrative phase if cooperative, distributive if adversarial.
    3. Tentative agreement — a proposed contract is agreed between committee members; stewards promise to recommend it to the membership.
    4. Ratification vote — every employee in the bargaining unit has the right to vote. A contract cannot be accepted until a majority votes to accept it.
  • What a contract codifies — uniform wages, hours, working conditions. Management and union renegotiate improvements at defined intervals.
  • The failure path (Tepperman's) — May 1, 2001: stewards put the agreement to members. One member of the union's own negotiating committee campaigned against the proposal (contrary to his indication to the committee). Vote: 23 to 4 against. Next morning, 8:30 a.m. May 2: walkout. 10:00 a.m.: picket lines outside the Windsor store and warehouse. Management was completely surprised — they had been ready to return to the table.
  • Why the deal failed (the deeper read) — years of "we've always given in to what they offered us; finally we decided to say 'that's enough'". Internal divisions on the committee. A warehouse-director liaison who employees wanted fired but management would only commit to improving. Member buy-in was never built.
  • Exam move — diagnose ratification failure not as a process error but as a relational failure. The committee can negotiate; only the membership can ratify.
High-Performance Work Systems (HPWS) — the bundle Pfeffer · bundle approach Supporting
HPWS isn't one practice; it's a reinforcing bundle. The whole point is horizontal fit — each practice multiplies the others. Drop one and the system degrades.
  • The standard bundle (memorise these seven):
    1. Selective hiring — low offer-to-hire ratios; careful fit; you're going to invest a lot, so be picky.
    2. Extensive training — both initial and ongoing; technical and interpersonal; treated as an investment, not an expense.
    3. Self-managed teams — decentralised decision-making; teams own quality, scheduling, problem-solving.
    4. High contingent compensation — gainsharing, profit-sharing, equity; share gains broadly, not just at the top.
    5. Job security — explicit no-layoff or strong protection commitments; the precondition for everything else (without it, why share information, why train, why suggest improvements that might eliminate your job?).
    6. Information sharing — open books on financial and operational data; two-way communication; transparency about strategy.
    7. Reduced status differences — single dining rooms, no reserved parking, similar dress codes, narrower pay spreads (echoes the Block 6 egalitarian-compression argument).
  • Horizontal fit (the load-bearing point) — these practices must reinforce each other. Selective hiring without training wastes the selection. Training without job security trains people for your competitors. Self-managed teams without information sharing flies blind. Contingent comp without information sharing feels arbitrary. The bundle is the unit of analysis — not any single practice.
  • The fragility problem — HPWS is expensive up-front. Under cost pressure, the temptation is to cut one practice (usually training, then security). But cutting one degrades the others. This is exactly why Pfeffer argues union contracts matter — they lock in the bundle.
  • Connection to Block 1 (AMO) — HPWS is the operational expression of AMO. Selective hiring + training build Ability; contingent comp + security + status reduction drive Motivation; teams + information sharing create Opportunity.
  • Exam move — when asked to design or critique a high-commitment system, name the bundle, then defend horizontal fit, then explain why a contract is the durability mechanism.

The cases worth remembering

  • Ford / UAW (Petersen, 1980s) — CEO Donald Petersen implemented total quality management and transformed Ford's culture. He credited his ability to persevere through setbacks to the UAW's presence — the changes were embedded in the formal contract, which gave them durability.
  • Saturn (GM) & the UAW — at one point a model of union-management cooperation and a high-productivity producer of high-quality cars. People at Saturn credited the contract with maintaining the cooperative, high-performance practices.
  • Kaiser-Permanente partnership (1997-) — $28B HMO. Joint decision-making across 26 local unions. Injuries down >20%, employee sat 65% → 80%, member sat +3%, cost savings >$100M. The strongest "cooperative bargaining works" case.
  • 344 California hospitals (2004 study) — risk-adjusted heart-attack mortality 5.5% lower at facilities with unionized nurses (post-controls). Mechanism: nurse power + joint staffing decisions. Adversarial relations destroy the gain.
  • Southwest Airlines & UPS — the most-unionized U.S. airline has been profitable each of the past 35 years. UPS, also heavily unionized, is among the most competitive in its sector. Falsifies "unions kill competitiveness."
  • Cingular Wireless & CWA — neutral while 18,000 employees organized; simultaneously posted the best financial results in its history. CWA even launched a campaign to market Cingular's service. "Engaged employees rather than nursing grudges" (VP HR Lew Walker).
  • Tepperman's (Ivey 9B03C028, 2001) — the counter-case. Family-owned Windsor home furnishings retailer, "Somebody Cares" culture since the 1920s, ~300 employees, 35 unionized warehouse staff. Tentative deal end of April 2001; ratification May 1: 23-4 against. Walkout 8:30 a.m. May 2; picket lines by 10:00 a.m. Escalated to nails on the parking lot. Bill Tepperman: "Never before in 75 years was it 'us versus them.'" Diagnostic value: shows ratification as the real bottleneck, the warehouse-director liaison failure, and how a single committee member campaigning against a deal can derail it.

Exam moves

  • Frame any union question as High Road vs Low Road. The high road needs HPWS; HPWS needs durability; unions provide it. That's the spine of any pro-union essay.
  • Dismantle "unions kill profits" with Pfeffer's feedback loop. Walk the four steps: skill in, turnover out, capital deepening, training. Total cost roughly unchanged; productivity higher. Add the monopoly-power boundary condition.
  • Defend partnership-style bargaining over adversarial. Adversarial shrinks joint gains. Cite Kaiser-Permanente (injuries, satisfaction, cost), the 5.5% mortality study, Cingular. Then cite Tepperman's as the negative case.
  • Argue unions sustain HPWS by locking the bundle into contractual language. Without the contract, training and security are first to go in a downturn. With it, they survive. Petersen at Ford is the cleanest illustration.
  • When inequality comes up, deploy egalitarian compression. Unions narrow race/gender gaps and compress dispersion; the decline in unionization tracks rising inequality; wide pay spreads also harm performance (baseball, faculty, top teams).
  • Treat bargaining as power-sharing, not a zero-sum price negotiation. Frame it as institutionalised communication that gives front-line staff voice on operational matters they know better than head office.
  • Open any pro-union essay by naming the conventional wisdom — "management gets the union it deserves" — then dismantle it on three counts (complementary protections, persistence of historical gains, competitive firms that are unionized).
  • If asked to diagnose a bargaining failure, walk the process arc: committee → opening → bargaining → tentative deal → ratification. Failure is usually at ratification because committee buy-in ≠ member buy-in. Tepperman's 23-4 vote.
  • When critiquing HPWS, lean on horizontal fit. Don't list practices in isolation. Show how dropping one (usually training or security) degrades the others, and why the contract is the durability mechanism.
  • Don't oversell. Pfeffer himself notes not all unions are honest and enlightened — "but neither are all managements; in case you have forgotten WorldCom, Tyco, Enron." The honest claim is that contractual mechanisms make management backsliding less likely, not impossible.
