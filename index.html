§ 0 — The big picture

Two maps, one engine

Almost every framework in this course refines two ideas: how HRM connects to outcomes, and what makes people perform. Anchor yourself here first — the rest is detail.

The engine — AMO

A × M × O is multiplicative: any zero kills performance. The whole course slots into one of the three legs.

The map — Harvard Model

Multistakeholder, "soft" HRM. Stakeholders shape policies → policies produce 4 C's → which produce well-being for individuals, the firm, and society. The Michigan model is the harder, shareholder-only counterpart.

Chapter one · Block 1

01Foundations: SHRM, engagement & commitment

Before HRM is a system, it's a stance. What does the firm owe its people? What do its people owe back?

The block introduces the two grand maps of HRM (Harvard vs Michigan), the spine that makes any HR system work (AMO), and the constructs that explain why people show up and stay engaged (commitment + engagement + motivation). The threads tie together through a clear claim: HRM is a system of choices, and the choices have consequences far beyond profit.

Mindmap — the territory of Block 1
The HR system
The two grand maps
  • Harvard — multistakeholder, 4 C's
  • Michigan — matching, HR cycle
  • Hard ↔ Soft — the central debate
The performance spine
  • AMO — A × M × O (multiplicative)
  • Any zero = zero performance
  • The whole course = one of the three legs
Why people care
  • Engagement — Vigor · Dedication · Absorption
  • Commitment — Affective · Normative · Continuance
  • ToMo — direct vs indirect motives
The deeper "why"
  • SDT — autonomy · competence · relatedness
  • SET — reciprocal obligations
  • COR — resource spirals
HRM's broader claim
  • Beer/Boselie/Brewster — multistakeholder thesis
  • POS — perceived support → engagement
  • DREAMS — what makes a workplace work
The dark side
  • UPB — engaged people lying for the firm
  • Too much commitment ≠ better

The frameworks worth knowing cold

Harvard Model Beer, Spector, Lawrence, Mills & Walton (1984) Core
A multistakeholder map: situational forces + stakeholder interests shape HR policy choices → HR outcomes → long-term well-being.
  • Four policy areas — employee influence, HR flow, reward systems, work systems
  • Four C's (HR outcomes) — Commitment, Competence, Congruence, Cost-effectiveness
  • Long-term consequences — individual, organizational and societal well-being
  • Move — the lens to redesign whole HR systems (Ryanair); pair with the Beer/Boselie/Brewster multistakeholder critique
Total Motivation (ToMo) Doshi & McGregor (HBR) Core
Six motives: three direct (lift performance), three indirect (destroy it).
  • ToMo score — weighted index from -100 to +100 that predicts performance and customer outcomes
  • Move — cite when explaining culture-as-performance-engine; contrast with extrinsic-only Michigan logic
Engagement vs Commitment — be precise Schaufeli & Bakker / Meyer & Allen Core
Two different constructs. Engagement is a state; commitment is a bond.
  • Engagement (VDA) — Vigor (energy), Dedication (pride/inspiration), Absorption (flow)
  • Commitment (3-component) — Affective (want to), Normative (ought to), Continuance (have to)
  • Best outcomes — Affective commitment. Continuance traps people; normative is mixed
  • Move — never conflate the two. If asked about "engagement", give Schaufeli; if about "why people stay", give Meyer & Allen
"HRM should serve a wide set of stakeholders — shareholders, employees, unions, community and government — and balance economic, individual and societal well-being rather than only shareholder returns." Beer, Boselie & Brewster · 2015 · the multistakeholder thesis

The debate to lead with — Hard vs Soft HRM

  • Hard (Michigan, Fombrun et al.) — instrumental, shareholder-driven, matching model. HR follows strategy.
  • Soft (Harvard, Beer et al.) — developmental, multistakeholder, Four C's. HR shapes strategy too.
  • Where you land — most exam answers should open by declaring a stance, even if you go nuanced after.

Exam moves

  • Anchor any whole-HRM essay on either Harvard or Michigan — declare your stance early.
  • Cite AMO to explain any failure to perform — it's multiplicative, so the bottleneck is always one of A, M, or O.
  • Distinguish engagement (state, VDA) from commitment (bond, 3-component). Mix them up and the examiner notices.
  • Use UPB to challenge "more engagement = better" — too much commitment fuels unethical behavior for the firm.
Chapter two · Block 2

02Designing the role & planning the workforce

Once HRM has a stance, design follows. A great person in a broken job still fails — so design the job first, then plan who fills it.

Block 2 covers four moves that come before hiring anyone: designing the job (Simons, Hackman & Oldham), analysing the job (KSAOs), planning the workforce (demand vs supply), and structuring recruitment and selection (Armstrong's process). The iconic visual is Simons' four sliders.

Mindmap — from role design to hiring decision
Designing roles & choosing people
Job design
  • Simons' Four Spans — Control + Support (supply) vs Accountability + Influence (demand)
  • JCM — 5 characteristics → 3 states → outcomes
  • Job breadth — enrichment / enlargement / rotation
Job analysis
  • Description — what the job is
  • Specification — what the person must be
  • KSAOs (Campion) — Knowledge, Skills, Abilities, Other
Workforce planning
  • Right person, right job, right time, right cost
  • Demand > Supply — hire / train / retain / overtime
  • Demand < Supply — freeze / redeploy / voluntary exit / layoff
Recruitment
  • Internal — cheap, cultural fit, creates a new vacancy
  • External — fresh skills, costly, no track record
  • Employer brand / EVP — the firm sells itself too
Selection (6 steps)
  • Application → tests → interviews → references → physical → offer
  • RACI — clarify ownership at every step
Layoffs (when needed)
  • Seniority · Status · Skill · Merit · Personal
  • Defend the choice with the criterion

The frameworks worth knowing cold

Simons' Four Spans of Job Design Simons (HBR) Core
Design jobs so the supply of resources matches the demand on the role — sliders form an "X" when balanced.
  • Supply — Span of Control (resources commanded) + Span of Support (help available)
  • Demand — Span of Accountability (metrics measured) + Span of Influence (cross-unit cooperation needed)
  • Crisis diagnosis — demand > supply = crisis of resources; supply > demand = crisis of control
  • Move — the central Block 2 tool. Map any "broken role" question to the four sliders
Job Characteristics Model Hackman & Oldham Core
Five job features create three psychological states that drive motivation, satisfaction and effectiveness.
Workforce Planning — demand vs supply Lepak & Gowan Core
Right person, right job, right time, right cost. The action menu depends on which way the gap runs.
  • Demand > Supply — external recruitment, training/upskilling, retention bonuses, overtime
  • Demand < Supply — hiring freeze, internal redeployment, voluntary exit programs, layoffs
  • Step zero — analyse the current workforce + forecast both sides BEFORE choosing an action
  • Move — the frame for PowerTech India (growth) and SolidTech (downsize) cases

The cases

  • Ricci & Verona — write the job description and specification; what KSAOs matter?
  • PowerTech India — demand > supply scenario; the recruitment menu
  • SolidTech — demand < supply; criteria for layoff selection

Exam moves

  • Diagnose any "broken role" by mapping it to Simons' four sliders — what's the missing supply or excess demand?
  • Use JCM to defend job enrichment over training/hiring when motivation is the bottleneck.
  • For any workforce-planning case, name the direction (Demand > or < Supply) first, then pull from the matching action menu.
  • Cite the validity hierarchy: structured interviews + ability tests beat unstructured chat and GPA (you'll need this in Block 3 too).
Chapter three · Block 3

03From candidate to colleague — selection, socialization, training

Now you fill the role. The principle: hire for what you can't train, train what you can — and remember that culture fit can be socialized but skill mismatch usually can't.

The block runs from defining the requirement (Armstrong) through measurement (validity, Big Five, cognitive tests), through onboarding (the three domains), to training (process model, Kirkpatrick). Cappelli's blunt verdict on unstructured interviews is the kind of cite that wins exam points.

Mindmap — building the workforce
From candidate to colleague
Selection arc (Armstrong)
  • Define → Attract → Process → Select → Decide → Offer
  • Phases — Measurement → Decision → Evaluation
What actually predicts
  • GMA (general mental ability) — #1 single predictor
  • Conscientiousness — #1 personality predictor
  • Experience → self-efficacy
  • Cappelli — unstructured interviews + GPA are weak
P-O vs P-J fit
  • P-O = culture (socializable)
  • P-J = skills (harder to retrain)
  • Over-P-O → homogeneity
Interview types
  • Structured > unstructured (validity)
  • Situational — hypothetical, for inexperienced
  • Behavioral (STAR) — past behavior, for experienced
Onboarding
  • 3 domains — Organization · Group · Task
  • Strategies — sequential · individual · collective
  • Skip task domain → role clarity collapses
Training & development
  • Process — Needs → Design → Implement → Evaluate
  • Kirkpatrick — Reactions · Learning · Behavior · Results
  • Training ≠ development (current vs future)

The frameworks worth knowing cold

Predictor hierarchy Judge et al. (2007) meta-analysis Core
Decades of evidence rank-order what actually predicts on-the-job performance — and tools you can't drop are surprisingly few.
  • Move — defend cognitive tests + conscientiousness over interviews and grades. Cite Cappelli (2019) to dismiss what most firms actually do.
Three domains of socialization Block 3 deck Key
Onboarding works in three layers — skip one and integration breaks.
  • Organization — values, mission, history, policies
  • Group/Department — supervisor expectations, peer norms, team expertise
  • Task — responsibilities, performance expectations, resources/tools
  • Move — diagnose any onboarding failure by which layer was skipped
Training Process Model Block 3 deck Key
A four-step cycle. Skip the first step and you design for the wrong gap.
  • Kirkpatrick levels — Reactions (liked it?) → Learning (knew it?) → Behavior (used it?) → Results (org impact?)
  • Move — argue past Level 1; show how to evaluate behavior change and ROI
"Unstructured interviews and college grades are weak predictors of job performance — yet they're what most firms rely on. The fix isn't to add more interviews; it's to add structure and use ability tests." Cappelli · 2019 · the predictor heresy

Exam moves

  • Open any selection question with the predictor hierarchy + Cappelli — the easiest way to look well-read.
  • Use P-O vs P-J fit to argue both diversity and skill — over-selecting for fit produces homogeneous teams.
  • Cite Kirkpatrick to push training evaluation past Level 1 (satisfaction surveys are not evaluation).
  • Diagnose onboarding failures by which of the three domains (org/group/task) got skipped.
Chapter four · Block 4

04Performance management & appraisal

Once people are in their roles, how do you tell them how they're doing — and what good looks like? Aguinis' framing is the cite that powers the whole block.

Block 4's central debate is sharper than its title: most firms do appraisal (once a year, HR-driven, past-focused) and call it management. The interesting design choices live in how you measure (BARS, BOS, MBO/SMART, 360, forced distribution), against what (traits, behaviors, outcomes), and against whom (absolute vs relative judgment).

Mindmap — measuring & developing performance
Performance management
The central frame
  • Aguinis — Identify · Measure · Develop
  • The debate — Appraisal (annual, past) vs Management (continuous, future)
  • Two uses — administrative · developmental
What to measure
  • Traits — risky (bias-prone)
  • Behaviors — observable on the job
  • Outcomes — results delivered
How to measure
  • BARS — behavior-anchored numeric scales
  • BOS — frequency of behaviors
  • MBO + SMART — outcomes vs jointly-set goals
  • Mixed Standard — keyed good/avg/poor
Against whom
  • Absolute — vs fixed standards
  • Relative — ranking / forced distribution
  • Vitality Health: shift to relative
Who rates
  • 360 feedback — peers, reports, supervisor
  • Risky in low-trust + high-stakes contexts
  • Alex Sander case
Rater errors
  • Halo · Leniency · Central tendency
  • Contrast · Recency · Similar-to-me
  • Fix — frame-of-reference training

The frameworks worth knowing cold

Appraisal vs Management — the Aguinis dial Aguinis, Joo & Gottfredson (2011) "Why we hate PM" Core
Two ends of one dial. Most firms sit at the appraisal end and wonder why nobody likes the system.
  • The diagnosis — "we hate PM" usually means "we hate appraisal pretending to be management"
  • Deloitte — replaced ratings with weekly check-ins + four future-focused statements (e.g. "I'd always want this person on my team")
  • Move — whenever a case asks "why is the PM system failing?", diagnose which end of the dial it sits on
Forced distribution — the Vitality Health move Bingham & Beer · case 913-501 Core
A bell curve forces differentiation. Fixes inflation, breaks team cohesion. Use with care.
  • Vitality redesign — 13 absolute rating levels → 5 relative categories + job-specific competencies + SMART objectives + common January cycle + short/long-term cash + equity
  • Risk — a high-talent team is still forced to label someone "low"
  • Move — defend or attack based on team-level effects, not just system-level inflation
"Performance management is not appraisal. It is a continuous process of identifying, measuring and developing the performance of individuals and teams — and aligning it with the strategic goals of the organization." Aguinis et al. · 2009

Rater errors to name (and how to fix)

  • Halo — one trait colors all ratings · Leniency / Severity — too soft / too hard
  • Central tendency — clusters everyone in the middle · Contrast — judging vs others just rated
  • Recency — over-weights last few months · Similar-to-me — favors people like the rater
  • Fix — frame-of-reference training calibrates raters and curbs systematic errors

Exam moves

  • Cite Aguinis to reframe "we hate PM" as "we do appraisal poorly". The fix is to move toward the management end of the dial.
  • For any rating-design case, name what's being measured (trait/behavior/outcome) AND how (absolute vs relative).
  • Use Vitality Health as your go-to case for moving from absolute to relative ranking — but flag the team-cohesion cost.
  • Argue 360 feedback only where culture supports honest peer feedback (Alex Sander = it didn't).
Chapter five · Block 5

05Pay, incentives & employment relations

Pay is the loudest signal. Who you attract, who you keep, what they do — it's downstream of design choices about money.

Block 5 hands you Milkovich's Pay Model (the foundational diagnostic), the Total Returns split (cash is only part of the package), and Gerhart & Rynes' under-cited insight that pay also sorts who walks in the door — not just how hard they work once inside. The cross-country comparator (Hall & Soskice's VoC) explains why what works in Germany doesn't work in Texas.

Mindmap — pay design & employment relations
Pay & rewards
The foundational model
  • Pay Model (Milkovich) — objectives · policies · techniques
  • Objectives — Efficiency · Fairness · Ethics · Compliance
  • Policies — Internal · External · Contributions · Management
The reward mix
  • Total Returns — transactional + relational
  • Direct vs Indirect compensation
  • WorldatWork — the practitioner version
Pay for performance
  • Merit — base lift (sticky)
  • Incentive — re-earned, variable
  • BAG Grocers + Zero Wage cases
Two pay effects
  • Incentive effect — motivates who you have
  • Sorting effect — changes who you attract
  • Gerhart & Rynes: glass-installation study
Pay structures
  • Narrow-graded — hierarchical
  • Broad-graded — flatter orgs
  • Job-family — by occupation
Country context
  • Hall & Soskice — CMEs vs LMEs
  • Likelihood-to-unionize matrix
  • Caveat Emptor — useful? causation? alternatives?

The frameworks worth knowing cold

The Pay Model — three layers Milkovich, Newman & Gerhart Core
Objectives drive policies, which drive techniques. Any pay system can be diagnosed against this stack.
Sorting effect vs Incentive effect Gerhart & Rynes Core
Pay shapes performance through two mechanisms. Most discussions forget the second one — and Gerhart's evidence says it's the bigger lever.
  • Evidence — 44% productivity rise in the glass-installation piece-rate study; the majority came from sorting, not incentive
  • Move — argue pay design changes who you have, not only how hard they work. The killer counter-move to "money doesn't motivate"
Total Returns — the full reward mix Ch1 Pay Model Core
Reward = cash + benefits + relational returns. Pay alone is a thin lever.
  • Total Compensation (transactional) — base · merit/COLA · short and long-term incentives · benefits
  • Relational/Psychological Returns — recognition · employment security · challenging work · learning · leadership opportunities
  • Move — when pay alone can't fix a retention problem, design the whole return mix
"The glass-installation firm raised productivity 44% by shifting to piece-rate. Most of that gain came not from existing workers trying harder — but from better workers self-selecting in and weaker ones leaving." Gerhart & Rynes · the sorting story

Exam moves

  • Open any compensation question by naming the Pay Model's three layers (objectives → policies → techniques) — instant structure.
  • Distinguish merit (base lift, sticky) from incentive (re-earned, variable) every time pay-for-performance comes up.
  • Invoke the sorting effect whenever someone says "but money doesn't really motivate". The data disagrees.
  • Use VoC (Hall & Soskice) to explain why "the right" pay system is country-specific. German practice ≠ US practice.
Chapter six · Block 6

06Unions, collective bargaining & the high road

Pfeffer's quiet provocation: good HR doesn't replace unions. Sometimes it needs them.

Block 6 inverts the conventional wisdom. The standard claim ("good management makes unions unnecessary") becomes the thesis Pfeffer dismantles. Unions raise wages — but feedback effects (skill, lower turnover, capital investment) often offset the cost. And contracts lock in high-commitment practices that would otherwise erode under cost pressure.

Mindmap — unions, bargaining & strategy
Unions & HPWS
The reframe (Pfeffer)
  • Conventional wisdom — "good HR forestalls unions"
  • Pfeffer's challenge — even good HR doesn't make unions redundant
  • Unions can sustain HPWS
Union wage effects
  • Wages rise — but feedback effects offset
  • Skilled workers attracted
  • Lower turnover · capital investment
  • Profit hit bites mainly under monopoly
High road / Low road
  • Low — compete on cost (unwinnable for advanced economies)
  • High — compete on innovation + productivity via HPWS
  • Union backing makes high road durable
HPWS + unions
  • Contracts entrench training, teams, security
  • Cases: Ford · Saturn · Kaiser-Permanente
  • Prevents "backsliding" under cost pressure
Bargaining
  • Power-sharing & joint problem-solving
  • Negotiation → tentative deal → ratification vote
  • Tepperman's: 23-4 rejection
Inequality angle
  • Egalitarian ethos compresses wages
  • Lower discrimination by race/gender
  • Wide dispersion harms performance

The frameworks worth knowing cold

Pfeffer's feedback loop Pfeffer (drawing on Freeman & Medoff) Core
Higher union wages → skilled workers attracted → lower turnover → capital investment → productivity rises → wage cost offset.
  • Boundary condition — union profit-effects matter mainly where firms hold monopoly power
  • Move — defend that the wage-vs-profit trade-off is overstated; cite the feedback mechanisms
High Road vs Low Road Pfeffer Core
Two competitive paths. Only one is sustainable for advanced economies — and union backing makes it durable.
"Unionization is not antithetical to but positively associated with high-commitment work practices. The contracts lock in training, teams, job security, information sharing — preventing the backsliding that would otherwise occur under cost pressure." Pfeffer · the reframe

The cases worth remembering

  • Ford / Saturn / Kaiser-Permanente — union + HPWS coexistence and synergy
  • Kaiser-Permanente partnership — cooperative bargaining → lower patient mortality (unionized nurses)
  • Tepperman's — tentative deal rejected 23-4 by members; back to bargaining (or strike)

Exam moves

  • Frame any unions question as a "low road vs high road" strategic choice. The high road needs HPWS; HPWS needs durability; unions provide it.
  • Cite Pfeffer's feedback effects to dismantle "unions kill profits" — the trade-off is mediated by productivity gains.
  • Defend partnership-style labor relations (Kaiser-Permanente) over adversarial bargaining — adversarial shrinks the joint gains.
  • Use the egalitarian-ethos argument when an essay drifts to inequality — unions compress dispersion, which often improves performance.
§ Closing · The authors timeline

Six decades of HRM thought

Where the ideas came from. Anchor a citation by decade — examiners notice when you know when an idea entered the field.

1960s — foundations

  • Blake & Mouton — the Managerial Grid (people vs task)
  • Drucker — Management by Objectives (MBO)

1970s — job design

  • Hackman & Oldham — Job Characteristics Model + MPS

1980s — the maps appear

  • Beer, Spector, Lawrence, Mills & Walton (1984) — Harvard Model
  • Fombrun, Tichy & Devanna (1984) — Michigan Model
  • Deci & Ryan (1985) — Self-Determination Theory
  • Hobfoll (1989) — Conservation of Resources
  • Blanz & Ghiselli (1972) — Mixed Standard Scale (earlier, but Block 4)

1990s — measuring people

  • Meyer & Allen (1991) — Three-Component Commitment
  • Latham & Sue-Chan (1996) — Situational vs Behavioral interviews

2000s — comparative + meta-analytic

  • Hall & Soskice (2001) — Varieties of Capitalism (CMEs vs LMEs)
  • Schaufeli & Bakker (2004) — Work Engagement (Vigor · Dedication · Absorption)
  • Cropanzano & Mitchell (2005) — Social Exchange Theory in HRM
  • Judge et al. (2007) — Meta-analytic predictors of performance
  • Aguinis et al. (2009) — PM Cycle

2010s — the modern frame

  • Campion et al. (2011) — Competency modeling (KSAOs)
  • Aguinis, Joo & Gottfredson (2011) — "Why we hate PM"
  • Goffee & Jones (HBR 2013) — DREAMS / Best Workplace on Earth
  • Beer, Boselie & Brewster (2015) — Multistakeholder HRM
  • Cappelli (2019) — Weak predictors (interviews, grades)
  • Doshi & McGregor — Total Motivation (ToMo)
  • Pfeffer — Unions and HPWS (the Block 6 reading)

One caveat

The color coding throughout this companion (🔵 by block, importance signals in the cheat sheet) is a memory hook, not a fixed taxonomy. Importance shifts with the exam question — a "supporting" framework can be the killer cite if the prompt happens to be about it. Don't let the colour decide what you actually write.
